# ⛔ EXECUTIVE SUMMARY — WHAT YOU MUST DO TO STAY NON-RESIDENT

*The whole document in one page. Items 1 and 5 catch you **regardless of day count**; items 2, 3 and 4 are day-count tests.*

> ### Why this is worth obeying even in a year with no income
>
> **Becoming UK resident for one tax year makes it much harder to stay non-resident for the next three.**
>
> **The mechanism: there are two day-count tables, and one resident year moves you from the generous one to the harsh one.** Which table applies to you depends on a single question — *were you UK resident in any of the previous three tax years?*
>
> - **No** → you are an **"arriver"**, and the generous table applies. **This is you today**, and has been since you left in 2013.
> - **Yes** → you are a **"leaver"**, and the harsh table applies — **for three years after** the resident year, because the question looks back three years each time.
>
> **So one resident year costs you three years of the better table.** The effects compound:
>
> - **Today, on the generous table, you can spend up to 182 days here and still be non-resident.** On the harsh table the same facts would catch you at **121 days** — and if the extra connection below applies, at **46 days**. Your room shrinks from six months to six weeks.
> - **The "stay under 46 days and you're automatically safe" escape drops to under 16 days** — barely two weeks a year.
> - **A new connection may start counting against you:** it applies if the UK is where you spend more midnights than any other single country. *(It is not automatic — someone spending 90 days here and the rest in Hong Kong would not have it.)*
> - **Day trips start counting.** Right now, a day when you fly in and out without staying the night does not count at all. After a resident year, once you have three connections, those days start counting after the first thirty.
>
> **Full detail and both tables side by side are at §3.**
>
> **And the resident year itself would be a full year.** There is a relief that lets some people split a tax year into a resident part and a non-resident part — **you do not qualify for it** (§6.1). So a resident 2026/27 would tax you as a UK resident from **6 April 2026**, including the months you spent entirely in Hong Kong before arriving on 30 July.
>
> **It would also burn a valuable relief.** Because you have been away 12 years, your first four years of UK residence would be largely shielded from UK tax on your foreign income and gains. That four-year clock starts with your first resident year **whether or not you use it** — so an accidental resident year now, with no income to shelter, would waste a quarter of it. (Summary at §6.2.)

## ⭑ THE DECISION — what crossing 90 and 121 days actually costs

*The operative question: does Julian need to leave the UK before 90 days, or can he stay longer?*

### First, the four terms this section uses

Everything below rests on four ideas. They are the whole mechanism.

**1. The Statutory Residence Test (SRT)** decides whether you are UK tax resident in a given tax year (6 April to 5 April). It is mechanical, not a judgement call: you either meet a test or you do not.

**2. "Arriver" vs "leaver."** If you were **not** UK resident in any of the three preceding tax years you are an *arriver*, and the generous table applies. If you were resident in any of them you are a *leaver*, and a much stricter table applies, plus an extra fifth tie. **Julian and Sophia are both arrivers** (non-resident 2023/24, 2024/25, 2025/26). Losing arriver status is the single most expensive thing that can happen in this analysis.

**3. The four ties.** If no automatic test decides the question, residence turns on how many "ties" — connections to the UK — you hold, measured against your day count. An arriver has four available:

| Tie | You have it if... | Julian, 2026/27 |
|---|---|---|
| **Accommodation** | UK accommodation is available to you for a continuous 91 days and you spend at least 1 night there — **16 nights** if it is a close relative's home | ✅ **HELD** — living at his mother's, far beyond 16 nights |
| **Family** | A UK-resident spouse, cohabiting partner, or your own child under 18. **For a child there is a second limb: you must also see them in person in the UK on 61+ days** (see below) | ❌ No partner; and Sophia cannot be resident below 183 days |
| **Work** | You work 40+ days of more than 3 hours in the UK | ❌ Not working in the UK |
| **90-day** | You spent more than 90 days in the UK in **either** of the two preceding tax years | ❌ 39 days in 2025/26; under 45 in 2024/25 |

*(A fifth, the **country tie**, exists only for leavers. It does not apply to Julian while he remains an arriver.)*

**4. Table B — the arriver day-count table.** This is the table referred to throughout. **It answers one question only: am I UK resident for this tax year, yes or no?**

Being an arriver is an **input**, not an output — it is what selects Table B rather than the harsher Table A. The sequence is:

> **Were you resident in any of the last 3 tax years?** → No, so you are an **arriver** → therefore use **Table B** → **look up your UK day count against your number of ties** → the table tells you whether you are **resident or non-resident** this year.

So "arriver" is settled before you open the table; the table then decides residence.

| UK days in the tax year | Ties needed to make you UK RESIDENT |
|---|---|
| Up to 45 | — automatically **non-resident** (second automatic overseas test) |
| 46–90 | **All 4** |
| 91–120 | **3 or more** |
| 121–182 | **2 or more** |
| 183+ | — automatically **resident**, ties irrelevant |

> ### ⚠️ THERE ARE TWO DIFFERENT "90s" — AND THEY ARE UNRELATED
>
> The number 90 appears twice in this test, meaning two completely different things. Confusing them is the most likely way to misread this document.
>
> | | **The 90-day TIE** | **The 90-day BAND in Table B** |
> |---|---|---|
> | **What it is** | One of the four ties | A row boundary in the day-count table |
> | **Which year it looks at** | The **two PRECEDING** tax years | The **CURRENT** tax year |
> | **Question it asks** | Did I spend >90 UK days in either of the last two years? | How many UK days have I spent *this* year? |
> | **What it produces** | A **tie** — an input to the table | Nothing by itself — it just selects which row of the table you read |
> | **Direction** | Backward-looking | Present |
>
> **They are not connected.** You can spend 180 days in the UK this year and still have no 90-day tie this year (if the previous two years were quiet) — which is exactly Julian's position.
>
> **Worked through, for Julian in 2026/27:**
> - **The Table B band:** at 43 days (23 Sep 2026) he is heading into the 46–90 row, heading for 91–120 and possibly beyond. Those rows demand 4 ties and 3 ties respectively. **He has 1. So no band catches him** — the Table B 90 boundary is irrelevant to him this year, and so is the 121 boundary.
> - **The 90-day tie:** determined entirely by 2024/25 (under 45) and 2025/26 (39). **He does not have it, and cannot acquire it this year** — nothing he does between now and 5 April 2027 can change a test about two years that have already finished.
>
> > **So: the only reason 90 days matters to Julian at all is the TIE, not the TABLE.** The table's 90-day boundary does nothing to him. What matters is that crossing 90 days this year hands him a *tie* in 2027/28 and 2028/29 — which is a decision about those years, not this one.


> ### The single most important consequence
>
> **No band in Table B is triggered by one tie.** An arriver needs at least **two** ties before any day count below 183 can make them resident.
>
> So Julian's accommodation tie is, by itself, **completely inert**. One tie and zero ties give an identical answer: non-resident up to 182 days.
>
> **It only becomes dangerous when paired with a second tie** — and the 90-day tie is the one that would eventually pair with it.
>
> ### ⚠️ Critical: the 90-day tie looks BACKWARDS
>
> This is the single easiest thing to misread in the whole test. **The 90-day tie is never about the year you are in.** It asks only: *did I spend more than 90 days in the UK in either of the **two preceding** tax years?*
>
> Applied to Julian, year by year:
>
> - **2026/27 — the year he is in now.** The tie looks back at 2024/25 (under 45 days) and 2025/26 (39 days). Neither exceeds 90. **→ No 90-day tie, and he cannot acquire one this year whatever he does.**
>
>   *Why "cannot acquire"?* Because both of those tax years are **closed**: 2024/25 ended on 5 April 2025 and 2025/26 ended on 5 April 2026. Their day counts are settled history. No action available to Julian between now and 5 April 2027 can retroactively change how many days he spent in a year that finished eighteen months ago. The tie is therefore not merely absent this year — **it is unacquirable**. This is the asymmetry between the two "90s": the Table B band is about the current year and he can still move it by staying or leaving; the 90-day tie was decided before this tax year even began.
>
> - **2027/28.** The tie looks back at 2025/26 (39 days) and **2026/27 — this year**. So this year's count is what decides it. **→ 90-day tie only if he exceeds 90 days this year.**
>
> - **2028/29.** The tie looks back at **2026/27 — this year** and 2027/28. Either one exceeding 90 is enough. **→ 90-day tie if this year exceeds 90, or if next year does.**
>
> - **2029/30 onwards.** The tie looks back at 2027/28 and 2028/29. **This year has now dropped out of the window entirely.** → Whatever he does this autumn no longer has any effect.
>
> **So staying past 90 days this year does NOT give Julian a second tie this year.** He holds one tie in 2026/27 whatever he does, right up to 182 days. What it does is create a second tie in **2027/28 and 2028/29** — and it then expires.
>
> That is the entire substance of the 90-day question: **crossing 90 days does not change 2026/27. It changes the two years after it.** (Other things *can* still change 2026/27 — see the trap section and the three risks below.)

*(Table A is the equivalent table for leavers, and is much harsher — 4 ties at 16–45 days, 3 at 46–90, 2 at 91–120, and just 1 above 120. It matters only if Julian or Sophia becomes resident and loses arriver status.)*

### Short answer: do I need to leave the UK before 90 days, or can I stay longer?

**No — you do not need to leave before 90 days.** Crossing 90 days does not change your 2026/27 residence status; it affects only 2027/28 and 2028/29, and on the Hong Kong-job plan the cost is headroom you are unlikely to use. The reasoning follows.

#### On the 121-day threshold

**121 matters in future years, not in this one.**

- **In 2026/27 it does nothing.** You hold **one tie — the accommodation tie**, from living at your mother's house (close relative, 16-night threshold, long since passed). The other three do not apply: no family tie (no partner, and Sophia cannot be resident below 183 days), no work tie (not working in the UK), no 90-day tie (39 days in 2025/26, under 45 in 2024/25). Table B only makes an arriver resident above 120 days at **two or more** ties, so you pass straight through 121 and remain non-resident to 182 days. Full working at §3.
- **Crossing 121 rather than stopping at 91 costs you nothing extra for the future either.** The forward consequence is the 90-day tie, and that is created the moment you pass 90. Days 91 through 182 add no further forward damage.
- **But in 2027/28 and 2028/29, 121 becomes a real cliff** — *if* you cross 90 this year. You would then hold two ties (accommodation + 90-day), and at two ties Table B makes you resident above 120 days.

> ⚠️ **So do not read "121 doesn't matter" as a general rule.** It is true only of 2026/27. Carrying it into 2027/28 after crossing 90 this year is exactly how you would become UK resident without noticing.

**90 days is therefore the only threshold you control this year** — and crossing it affects exactly **two** future years, 2027/28 and 2028/29. It drops out entirely from 2029/30.

**Whether that is material depends on one thing: how many UK days you actually want in those two years.** On the Hong Kong-job-plus-visiting plan, it is probably immaterial.

### ⚠️ THE BIGGEST TRAP — you already meet three of the four conditions for automatic UK residence

**Read this before anything else about day counts.** Everything else in Part One is about *headroom* — how many days you can spend before ties catch you. This is different: it is a route to being **automatically UK resident at any day count**, and it is the most likely way this analysis goes wrong.

#### The second automatic UK test has four conditions. Julian meets three.

| | Condition | Julian |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | You have a home in the UK | ✅ **MET** — his mother's house |
| **2** | You are present in that home on **30+ days** in the tax year | ✅ **MET** — he is at ~43 |
| **3** | There is a **91-consecutive-day window**, 30+ days of it inside the tax year, throughout which you held that UK home | ⏳ **NOT YET — completes 28 Oct 2026.** Held since 30 July |
| **4** | **Throughout that window you had no usable overseas home** — meaning no overseas home at all, or none you are present in on 30+ days | ❌ **NOT MET** — the Hong Kong flat |

**Once condition 3 completes on 28 October, only condition 4 will be protecting him.**

#### Why this is a trap

**Conditions 1–3 are easily met, and carry no warning signs.** Anyone who *lives* with family in the UK for a few months meets all three without doing anything unusual:
- Staying at a parent's house for months makes it a home
- 30 days of presence is six weeks
- A 91-day holding window is just having arrived three months ago

They are not the test's discriminator and they carry no warning signs. **A person can meet three of four conditions for automatic UK residence simply by living at their mother's house for a few months.**

*(One qualification: conditions 1 and 3 need the UK place to be a **home**, and para 25(3) excludes "a holiday home or temporary retreat". A short summer visitor whose real life is abroad usually would not have a UK home at all. The point holds for someone living with family for months — which is Julian's position — but not for every visitor.)*

**Condition 4 is the discriminator, by design.** This is often called the *"only home" test*. Its target is someone who has relocated their life to a UK home but keeps their day count low. It asks: *is your UK home your only real home?* Having a genuine, used home abroad is the normal and intended way not to be caught — Julian is outside the population the test was written for, not scraping past on a technicality.

#### Why it matters so much THIS year

> **In 2026/27 the Hong Kong flat is the ONLY thing preventing automatic UK residence under this test. There is no fallback.**
>
> The other Stage 1 escapes are gone:
> - **Under 46 days** — he was at 43 on 23 Sep 2026 and is staying, so this expires on 26 September 2026
> - **Full-time work overseas** — unavailable for 2026/27, defeated by the significant break (§5.7)
>
> **If the flat stopped being a home, he would be UK resident for 2026/27 regardless of his day count.** The entire 90 / 120 / 182 analysis would become irrelevant — those thresholds only matter at Stage 3, and this test decides at Stage 2, before ties are ever examined.

#### How likely is it to fail? Low — and it is the primary dependency, not the only one

The supporting facts are strong: his home for 5 years; he lived in it for the **first 115 days of this tax year**; he returned and used it in September; **~127 days present against a 30-day requirement**; it is **empty and not let**; and he intends to return to live there. HMRC's guidance treats an empty, available overseas property as still a home even where the owner spends the *entire* year elsewhere — Julian is stronger than that, because he actually used it.

**The main action that would break it is letting the flat.** In HMRC's own example, Rosa let her New Zealand house commercially for 92 days; for that period it was not her home, which opened exactly this window and made her UK resident. That failure mode is entirely within Julian's control.

> ⚠️ **Two qualifications from adversarial review (24 Sep 2026):**
> 1. **A brief let is not automatically fatal.** The test needs **91 consecutive days** in which he has a UK home and no sufficiently-used overseas home. HMRC's Rosa example is a **92-day** commercial letting. A genuine short let, followed by restoration of a flat already used for ~127 days this year, does not by itself supply that window. The danger is a letting long enough to open one — not any letting at all.
> 2. **The Hong Kong flat is not the only thing capable of defeating this test.** It also requires a **UK home** throughout the window. If Julian genuinely ceased to use his mother's house as a home, the test would fail on that limb instead. Fact-sensitive and not to be relied on — but "there is no fallback" overstated it.

> **⚠️ The reversion trap — why qualification 1 is not a route.** The deficiency window closes only if the flat **reverts to being his home the moment the tenancy ends**. HMRC's guidance says a place becomes a home when the individual *actually uses it as one* (RFIG22140); Ivan's example implies reversion on the tenant leaving, **but Ivan returned to it**. If Julian is in the UK throughout, lets it for 60 days and never goes back, **whether home status resumes on the last day of the tenancy is genuinely open**. If it does not, the deficiency does not stop at 60 days — it keeps running, and 91 arrives five weeks later.
>
> **Three further reasons this is not a plan:** he does not control the duration (a tenant holding over, a notice period or a re-let turns 60 into 91+, and the test is binary); he does not control the characterisation dates (if HMRC saw home status lost slightly earlier or regained slightly later, 89 becomes 93); and there is no benefit that justifies the risk while he and Sophia are both in the UK.
>
> **Read qualification 1 as "do not panic if something short and unavoidable happens" — not as permission. The advice is unchanged: do not let the flat before 8 March 2027.**


#### ⚠️ Variant: what if he returns to Hong Kong but lets the flat and lives somewhere else?

*A live possibility, not a hypothetical. The answer is reassuring, but it turns on a point easily missed.*

**The overseas home does not have to be the flat he owns.** Condition 4 asks whether he has **an** overseas home — not that particular one. A **rented** Hong Kong flat he lives in is an overseas home: para 25(4) states a place counts as a home "whether or not P holds any estate or interest in it." Ownership is irrelevant.

So the scenario splits in two:

- ⚠️ **Lets the flat AND moves into another Hong Kong place he actually lives in.** He still has an overseas home — but **that is not sufficient on its own.**

  > **The replacement must itself clock 30+ presence days INSIDE 2026/27.** Condition B is judged at each moment of the window against the homes he then has, and para 8(5) counts presence "in year X". So a replacement home only defeats the test if he is present in it on 30 or more days **before 5 April 2027**.
  >
  > **Worked failure:** he lets the flat and moves into a rental on 20 February, then flies to the UK on 1 March and stays to 5 April. He has **9 presence days** in the rental. Condition B is therefore *satisfied* from 20 February; a window of 20 Feb – 21 May contains 45 days inside the tax year; **the test is met and he is UK resident at any day count.**
  >
  > **So the rule is: switch early enough, and stay.** A switch on 7 March leaves exactly 30 days available — no margin at all. Every UK trip after the switch eats into the count.

- ⚠️ **Lets the flat and has NO other overseas home** — between places, in hotels, staying casually with friends. For that period he has no overseas home at all, condition 4 flips to satisfied, and the test can bite.

> **The danger is not the letting — it is any window in which his only overseas home has fewer than 30 presence days in the tax year.** A gap with no overseas home is the obvious case, but an under-used replacement fails in exactly the same way.

#### ⚠️⚠️ AND SOPHIA NEEDS HER OWN REPLACEMENT — his does not cover her

**So if the owned flat is let before ~7 March 2027 while Sophia is still in the UK:**

5. Julian then has a **family tie** for the whole year (§ ties are assessed across the whole year)
6. With 2 ties and more than 120 days, **he is resident too**

> **The condition-4 test must be satisfied for each of them separately.** On the stated plan — returning to Hong Kong, letting the flat, living somewhere else before 5 April 2027 — **this is the live risk, and it runs through Sophia, not through Julian.**
>
> **If the flat is to be let while Sophia is still in the UK, she needs her own overseas home with 30+ presence days before 5 April 2027** — which in practice means she travels too, and stays long enough.

**Two things limit the damage even if a gap occurs:**

1. **Timing.** A gap only matters if it can host a 91-day window with 30+ days inside the tax year. **After 7 March 2027 no such window fits**, so a change made then or later cannot affect 2026/27.
2. **The UK home may end at the same time.** If he leaves his mother's house for good and makes no further use of it, that stops being his home too — and **condition 1 fails**, defeating the test by a different route. But this is fact-sensitive: if he would still stay there on visits, it likely remains his home, and he cannot rely on this.

**Forward implication.** Whichever Hong Kong property is his home must carry the **30+ days of presence in every tax year** in which he also has a UK home. If he moves to a rented place, the requirement transfers to that place — it is not satisfied by history in the old flat.

> **How the old flat's 127 days are treated — resolved, not an open question.** Para 8(6)(b) counts presence "at a time when it is a home of P's", so those days were validly counted while the flat was his home. Their effect is structural:
> - **Any window that includes even one pre-switch day fails**, because Condition B is not met on those days (127 presence days is far beyond the permitted 30)
> - **Any window starting on or after the switch is judged on the replacement home alone** — the old flat's history does nothing for it
>
> **So history protects earlier windows and is worthless for later ones.** This is why the switch date matters so much, and why the replacement's own 30 days are the whole game.

#### What to do about it

1. **Do not let or sub-let the Hong Kong flat**, even briefly, and do not let anyone else occupy it — **unless another Hong Kong home immediately replaces it** (see the variant above). Letting it with nothing to replace it is the one action that reliably breaks the defence
2. **Keep it available and unoccupied**, and keep returning to it
3. **Retain evidence of presence** — flights, utility bills, card transactions
4. **It must hold until 7 March 2027** for this year (§0); and it is an **annual** requirement, so the same must be true in every future year in which he has a UK home

Full analysis at §5.8; risk assessment at §9.7.

### The circumstance that DOES make it material: the family tie

#### First — how the family tie actually works for a child

A child gives you a family tie only if **both** of these are true. Either one failing means no tie:

1. **The child is UK resident in their own right** for that tax year — assessed by running the whole SRT on her separately; and
2. **You spend time with her in person, in the UK, on 61 or more days** in that tax year.

Three things about the 61-day count are worth knowing, because they decide how controllable it is:

- **Any part of a day counts.** Seeing her for an hour is a day. A day that starts together in the UK and ends on a flight to Hong Kong is still a UK contact day.
- **Days spent with her wholly outside the UK do not count at all** — not partially, not at a discount. Time together in Hong Kong, or on holiday anywhere that is not the UK, is unlimited and free.
- **It is your own diary**, not hers. It does not depend on her school calendar or her residence position.

**This is why limb 2 is the controllable one.** Limb 1 depends on Sophia's own circumstances, which once she is at UK boarding school are largely fixed. Limb 2 depends only on where you and she spend time together.

**For 2026/27 limb 2 is close to lost, but not lost yet.** Counting part-days: 30 July – 4 September is 37 contact days, plus 17–23 September is 7 — **44 contact days as at the end of 23 September 2026**, rising daily while they are together in the UK. The 61st falls on about **10 October 2026** if they remain together in the UK.

In practice it is not a lever, since avoiding it would mean separating from Sophia. But two things follow: the figure should be tracked rather than assumed, and **from 2027/28 onward the contact-day count becomes a genuine control** (§7), so the habit of counting it should start now.

Once limb 2 goes, this year's tie turns entirely on limb 1 — which is why her 183-day line on 28 January matters so much.

#### Why this matters for the 90-day decision

The 90-day tie is dangerous only in **combination**. If Sophia becomes UK resident **and** you see her in the UK on 61+ days, you pick up a family tie as your second or third tie:

| In 27/28, with Sophia UK resident | Your ties | Your ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| You stayed ≤90 this year, UK contact **under** 61 days | 1 | **182 days** |
| You crossed 90, UK contact **under** 61 days | 2 | **120 days** |
| You stayed ≤90, UK contact **61+** days | 2 | **120 days** |
| You crossed 90 **and** UK contact **61+** days | 3 | **90 days** |

**The controlling variable is the 61-day rule, not the 90-day tie.** Days spent with Sophia **wholly outside the UK do not count** ([RFIG22330](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig22330)). If she boards here and you live in Hong Kong, bring her to Hong Kong for holidays and keep UK-based contact under 61 days — and no family tie ever arises, whatever you do about the 90 days.

### If you take a Hong Kong job: a second, stronger protection that costs you nothing

*Corrected after peer review. An earlier draft framed this as a trade-off against the ties route. It is not — the two protections stack.*

**What this is about.** Everything above works through the ties test — count your days, count your ties, read Table B. But there is a completely separate route out, available only to someone working full-time abroad: the **third automatic overseas test**. If you meet it, you are non-resident immediately and the ties never get examined at all.

**Why it is a different kind of protection.** The SRT is applied as a sequence, not a balance:

> **Stage 1 — automatic overseas tests.** Meet any one, and you are **non-resident. Stop.** Nothing below is considered.
> **Stage 2 — automatic UK tests.** Meet any one, and you are **resident. Stop.**
> **Stage 3 — the ties test.** Only reached if Stages 1 and 2 are both silent. This is where Table B lives.

The work test sits at **Stage 1**, above everything discussed so far.

**Its conditions** (para 14(1), [RFIG20140](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig20140)): full-time work overseas across the tax year, no 31-day break from overseas work, fewer than **31 UK workdays**, and fewer than **91 UK days**.

**The point that was previously stated backwards.** Those limits look tighter than the 120- or 182-day ties ceiling, which made an earlier draft read as though you had to choose. You don't. **The tests are not alternatives.** If you meet the work test, you stop at Stage 1. If you fail it — say you spend 100 UK days — you simply fall through to Stage 3, where your ties ceiling applies exactly as it would have anyway.

**So holding the work test costs nothing and forfeits nothing. It adds a layer.** And it is the *better* layer, because Stage 1 non-residence does not depend on the Hong Kong flat still counting as a "home" — it removes that dependency entirely rather than relying on it holding. That flat is the one genuinely fact-sensitive point in this whole analysis, so a route that bypasses it is worth having.

The practical reading: **aim to meet the work test, and treat the ties ceiling as the backstop if a year's UK days run over 90.**

## 1. How the SRT actually works: a three-stage waterfall

This structure is the single most important thing to hold onto. The SRT is **not** a balance of factors. It is a sequence, and it stops at the first stage that answers.

| Stage | Test | If met |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Automatic **overseas** tests | **Non-resident. Stop.** Overrides everything below. |
| **2** | Automatic **UK** tests | **Resident. Stop.** Ties never considered. |
| **3** | Sufficient ties test | Day count × number of ties decides |

Two consequences that matter here:

- An automatic overseas test **beats** an automatic UK test. Under 46 days, nothing else can touch you.
- Defeating a test at one stage does **not** help at another stage. It only means you continue down the waterfall.

## 2. THE CONFLATION TRAP: "home" vs "accommodation"

**This is the easiest and most expensive mistake available in this analysis.** Both concepts involve a place to live. Both involve a figure of 30 days somewhere nearby. They are unrelated tests at different stages of the waterfall, and the Hong Kong flat answers one and is irrelevant to the other.

| | **Second automatic UK test** | **Accommodation tie** |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage** | 2 — automatic UK test | 3 — one of four ties |
| **Effect if met** | Automatically **resident** | Adds **one tie** |
| **Question asked** | Is your UK home effectively your *only* home? | Is UK accommodation available to you, and do you use it? |
| **Conditions** | UK home + present 30+ days + a 91-consecutive-day period (30+ days falling in the year) + **no meaningfully-used overseas home** | A place available for **91 continuous days** + at least 1 night (**16 nights** if a close relative's home) |
| **Does an overseas home help?** | ✅ **Yes — it is the escape hatch** | ❌ **No. Not mentioned in the test at all** |
| **Julian & Sophia** | **DEFEATED** — 30+ days in HK flat | **HELD** — both have it |
| **Source** | FA 2013 Sch 45 **para 8** (text at §5.8), [RFIG20330](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig20330), [RFIG20340](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig20340) | [RFIG20550](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig20550), [RFIG22170](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig22170), para 34 |

**Why they differ.** The second automatic UK test is about *exclusivity* — it targets people whose UK home is their only real home, so proving a genuine home elsewhere removes you from its scope. The ties are not tests of anything; they are **connection markers**. Owning a home in Hong Kong does not weaken your connection to your mother's house. You can have homes in five countries and still hold a UK accommodation tie.

**Practical rule: the Hong Kong flat bought an escape at Stage 2 only. It has no effect whatsoever at Stage 3.**

## 5. The next two years — what to avoid

### 5.1 The forward mechanism

#### What the 90-day tie is, and why it exists

**It is the test's memory.** The other three ties ask about your circumstances *now* — where you live, who is here, whether you work. The 90-day tie asks about your **recent past**.

**Why Parliament put it there** *(this paragraph is the document's own reading of the policy, not HMRC's stated rationale)*: connection to a country is not a single-year fact. Someone who spent five months here last year has a stronger continuing connection than someone arriving for the first time, even if this year's counts are identical. Without it, a person could alternate — 180 days one year, 20 the next — and be treated as freshly unconnected every other year. The tie stops that by carrying a pattern of presence forward for two years.

**The consequence for planning:** it is the only tie you cannot change during the year in question, and the only one where a decision made now lands later. That is what makes this section necessary at all.

#### How it works

The 90-day tie looks back at the **previous two tax years, each judged separately** ([RFIG20570](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig20570)). So the 2026/27 day count reaches forward into **2027/28 and 2028/29**, then drops out.

With 2025/26 confirmed at 39 days for both, **the 2026/27 count is now the sole determinant of the 90-day tie in 2027/28.**

**What this table shows.** §5.1 established that the 90-day tie looks backwards, so this year's day count becomes an input to the next two years. This table runs that forward: for each band of days Julian might spend in the UK this year, it gives his status **this** year, whether a 90-day tie is created, and what UK allowance he would then have in **2027/28 and 2028/29**.

### 5.4 The leaver ratchet — if either becomes resident

#### What it is, and why it exists

**Recent UK residence is itself treated as evidence of connection.** Someone who was UK resident last year and now claims to be non-resident is a different proposition from someone who has been abroad for a decade, and the SRT treats them differently on purpose.

**Why:** the test is designed to stop people flipping in and out of residence year by year — being resident when it suits, non-resident when it doesn't. So for anyone resident in the recent past it tightens on four fronts at once: a harsher day-count table, an extra tie, a much smaller automatic escape, and day-trips that suddenly count.

**Why it matters so much here:** Julian and Sophia are currently on the favourable side of this line, and have been since 2013. The ratchet is what makes a *single* resident year expensive out of all proportion to the tax due in that year — the cost is not the year, it is the three that follow.

#### How it works

Becoming UK resident in any year converts that person from "arriver" to **"leaver"** for the following **three** tax years:

- **Table A replaces Table B** — far stricter: 4 ties at 16–45 days, 3 at 46–90, **2 at 91–120, and just 1 above 120**
- **A fifth tie appears** — the **country tie**, available only to leavers, triggered only if the UK holds the greatest midnight count of any single country
- **The under-46-day escape disappears** — the second automatic overseas test requires non-residence in all three prior years. Only under 16 days, or full-time work abroad, would remain
- **Split-year relief in the ratchet years — available, contrary to an earlier draft.** *(Corrected 24 Sep 2026.)* Split-year **Cases 1–3 are departure cases and require UK residence in the previous tax year** — so they exist precisely for people in the ratchet. A leaver who starts full-time work overseas (Case 1), whose partner does (Case 2), or who ceases to have a UK home (Case 3) part-way through a ratchet year **can split that year**. This matters directly: if Julian became resident in 2026/27 and then took the Hong Kong job, **Case 1 could split 2027/28**. What is *not* available is a split of the **arrival** year 2026/27 itself (§6.1).
- **The deeming rule activates** ([RFIG20720](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig20720)) — with 3+ ties, day trips without a midnight start counting after the first 30

Concretely: accommodation + 90-day + country = 3 ties under Table A → **resident at 46 days**. Very hard to escape within three years.

### 5.5 The recurring obligation

**The 30 days in a Hong Kong home is an annual test, not a one-off.** There is no carry-over. For every tax year in which either person has a UK home, that person must be present in **an overseas home** on 30+ days *in that same tax year*.

> **Note: it need not be the flat he owns.** Condition 4 asks only whether he has *an* overseas home. A **rented** Hong Kong place he lives in qualifies — para 25(4): a place counts as a home "whether or not P holds any estate or interest in it." **The obligation attaches to whichever property is currently his home, and transfers if he moves.** History in a former home does not satisfy it.

In 2026/27 this happened incidentally — both were living in Hong Kong until 30 July. **From 2027/28 it must be deliberate**, and Sophia's 30 days will be constrained by UK school terms.

**Two structural requirements to protect:**
1. **Always have an overseas home while either has a UK home.** It carries the entire Stage 2 defence. Disposing of or letting the owned flat is safe **only if another Hong Kong home immediately replaces it** — the danger is a *gap* with no overseas home at all, not the letting itself (§5.8).
2. **Diarise both sets of 30 days annually**, built around school holidays.

### 5.7 A HONG KONG JOB — the third automatic overseas test

#### What it is, and why it exists

**It is the counterpart of the third automatic UK test (§5.9).** That one says: work full-time in the UK and you are resident whatever your day count. This one says: **work full-time abroad and you are non-resident, whatever your ties.** *(It still has day limits of its own — under 91 UK days and under 31 UK workdays — so it is not a route free of day counting.)*

*They are counterparts in purpose, not in mechanics:* the UK test runs over **any rolling 365-day period** with a condition that more than 75% of workdays are in the UK, while this one is assessed over **the tax year** with caps of fewer than 31 UK workdays and fewer than 91 UK days.

**Why Parliament provided it:** someone genuinely holding down a full-time job in another country has their life plainly centred there, and should not have to run a ties analysis to prove it. The test offers certainty in exchange for strictness — work abroad properly, keep UK presence modest, and you are out at Stage 1 with nothing further to consider.

**Why it is the best protection available to Julian:** it removes the dependency on how HMRC characterises the Hong Kong flat, which everything else in this document ultimately rests on (§5.8).

*Precisely stated:* it is **the only such route available in a year with more than 45 UK days.** Below 46 days the second automatic overseas test (§3) also bypasses Stage 2 entirely and needs no job at all — that is the route the boarding years rely on (§7.8).

#### How it works

If Julian works full-time in Hong Kong, the **third automatic overseas test** becomes available. This is a **Stage 1** escape: meet it and he is automatically non-resident regardless of ties, day-count bands, or the accommodation tie.

Conditions:
- Sufficient hours worked overseas (broadly a 35-hour weekly average) across the tax year
- No significant break — 31 days without a day of more than 3 hours' overseas work
- Fewer than **31 UK workdays** (more than 3 hours' work in the UK)
- Fewer than **91 days** in the UK in the tax year

**UNAVAILABLE for 2026/27 — decisively.** Two grounds, the first binary:

1. **Significant break (para 14(1)(b)) — fatal, and no amount of work can cure it.** The test requires that "during year X, there are no significant breaks from overseas work." A significant break is 31+ days with no day of 3+ hours' overseas work and no annual/sick/parenting leave (para 29(2)). Julian did no overseas work from 6 April to late September — far beyond 31 days. HMRC applies this to non-working periods outside any employment: the Alex example at [RFIG20140](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig20140) treats a 36-day gap as a significant break defeating the test.
2. **Sufficient hours (secondary).** The reference period stays at ~365 days — a period *before* a first employment is not a "gap between employments" and is not deductible (para 28(8), RFIG20160). That requires ~1,820 *actually worked* overseas hours; from a late-September start, roughly 67 hours a week.

> ⚠️ **CRITICAL FOR 2027/28.** Because the significant-break test runs across the whole tax year, a Hong Kong job must run **from 6 April 2027** with no 31-day non-leave gap. **A job starting 10 May 2027 fails the test for the entire 2027/28 year**, as does one that ends early. The start date is as important as the job itself.

**But it is the strongest available protection for 2027/28 onward.** A full tax year of full-time Hong Kong work, with UK days under 91 and UK workdays under 31, delivers automatic non-residence — no ties analysis, no accommodation tie problem, and no dependence on the Hong Kong flat remaining a "home" (§5.8). Structurally far more robust than day-counting.

> **⚠️ THE REMOTE-WORKING TRAP.** Para 27(1): *"Work is done where it is actually done, regardless of where the employment is held."* Doing Hong Kong-employer work **while physically in the UK** creates **UK workdays**. Those count toward the 40-day UK **work tie** and against the **31-day limit** in the third automatic overseas test. A Hong Kong contract worked remotely from the UK is a UK workday every time — and can destroy both protections at once.

### 5.8 THE HONG KONG FLAT MUST REMAIN A "HOME" — critical mechanism, low risk on current facts

*Added after peer review — both reviewers independently identified this as the weakest link. **Re-assessed 23 Sep 2026 and downgraded to LOW RISK** once the full facts emerged (§9.7): Julian lived in the flat for the first 115 days of 2026/27, returned to it in September, has ~127 days' presence against a 30-day requirement, keeps it empty and unlet, and intends to return to live there. The mechanism below remains load-bearing — if the flat ever stopped being a home, the consequence is automatic UK residence — but the characterisation is now well supported rather than uncertain.*

#### What this test is, and why it exists

**It is often called the "only home" test, and that name explains it.** Parliament's target was someone who has moved their life to a UK home but keeps their day count low — the person whose real base is a house here, but who flies out often enough to stay under the thresholds.

**So the test asks one question: is your UK home effectively your only home?** If it is, you are UK resident regardless of days. If you also have a genuine home elsewhere that you actually use, you fall outside the test entirely — which is the normal, intended outcome for someone living between two countries.

**Why that makes the Hong Kong flat load-bearing:** Julian satisfies every other limb simply by living at his mother's. The flat is the single fact keeping him outside a test he would otherwise meet — which is why its characterisation carries so much weight, and why this section exists.

#### How it works

The Stage 2 escape depends on **para 8(1)(c)(iii)**: "throughout that 91-day period, condition A or condition B is met". Condition A is "P has no home overseas."

**If the Hong Kong flat stops being a *home* at any point while a UK home is held, Condition A becomes satisfied from that date** — and a fresh 91-day window starting then, with 30+ days falling in the tax year, meets the test. Earlier presence does not save it (para 8(6)(b): presence counts only "at a time when it is a home of P's").

**Deadline: the flat must remain a statutory home until at least ~7 March 2027** — the last date from which a 91-day window still has 30 days inside 2026/27.

**Why retained ownership is not enough** — para 25:
> **25(3)** "somewhere that P uses periodically as nothing more than a holiday home or temporary retreat (or something similar) does not count as a home of P's."
> **25(5)** "Somewhere that was P's home does not continue to count as such merely because P continues to hold an estate or interest in it **after P has moved out** (for example, if P is in the process of selling it or has let or sub-let it, **having set up home elsewhere**)."

**HMRC's two directly relevant examples** ([RFIG20340](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig20340)):

- **Example 2 (Jane)** — had 30 days in her overseas home, but was still resident, because a separate 91-day window existed in which she had a UK home and *no* overseas home.
- **Example 5 (Rosa)** — let her New Zealand house commercially for 92 days; for that period it was not her home, creating the fatal window. **The closing counterfactual is the governing authority here:** *"If Rosa had not let out her New Zealand house, and it had remained available for Rosa to use throughout the summer; it would have remained her home and Rosa would not have met the second automatic UK test."*

> ### ⚠️ Two qualifications from adversarial review (24 Sep 2026)
> The mechanism above is right, but two earlier statements overstated it:
>
> **1. A brief let is not automatically fatal.** Para 8(1)(c) needs **91 consecutive days** in which Julian has a UK home and no sufficiently-used overseas home. HMRC's Rosa example is a **92-day** commercial letting — long enough to open such a window. **A genuinely short let, followed by restoration of a flat already used for ~127 days in the year, does not by itself supply one.** The danger is a letting long enough to create the window, not any letting at all. *(This is a reason not to panic over a short arrangement — not a licence to let. The risk scales with duration and the margin is unknowable in advance.)*
>
> **2. The Hong Kong flat is not the only thing capable of defeating this test.** Condition 1 requires a **UK home** throughout the window. If Julian genuinely ceased to use his mother's house as a home — moved out with no further use — the test would fail on that limb instead, whatever happened to the Hong Kong flat. **Fact-sensitive, and not something to rely on**, since occasional continued stays would likely preserve it as his home. But the earlier statement that "there is no fallback" was wrong.

> **⚠️ The reversion trap — why qualification 1 is not a route.** The deficiency window closes only if the flat **reverts to being his home the moment the tenancy ends**. HMRC's guidance says a place becomes a home when the individual *actually uses it as one* (RFIG22140); Ivan's example implies reversion on the tenant leaving, **but Ivan returned to it**. If Julian is in the UK throughout, lets it for 60 days and never goes back, **whether home status resumes on the last day of the tenancy is genuinely open**. If it does not, the deficiency does not stop at 60 days — it keeps running, and 91 arrives five weeks later.
>
> **Three further reasons this is not a plan:** he does not control the duration (a tenant holding over, a notice period or a re-let turns 60 into 91+, and the test is binary); he does not control the characterisation dates (if HMRC saw home status lost slightly earlier or regained slightly later, 89 becomes 93); and there is no benefit that justifies the risk while he and Sophia are both in the UK.
>
> **Read qualification 1 as "do not panic if something short and unavoidable happens" — not as permission. The advice is unchanged: do not let the flat before 8 March 2027.**

**Actions:**
1. **Do not let or sub-let the Hong Kong flat leaving nothing in its place** — while either person has a UK home. **Letting it is safe if another Hong Kong home immediately takes over** (see the variant below); it is a *gap* with no overseas home that is fatal. This is what destroyed Rosa's position.
2. **Keep it available and unoccupied by others.** Rosa's counterfactual and the Asif example (RFIG22140) both turn on continued availability.
3. **The 30 days does double duty** — it satisfies para 8(5) *and* evidences that the occupant has not "moved out" under para 25(5). Actual use is the best defence against re-characterisation.
4. **Sophia's exposure is higher in future years.** Once she is in UK school, holiday visits start to resemble para 25(3)'s "temporary retreat". Her characterisation may fail before Julian's.

**Note: there is no safety valve here.** The exceptional-circumstances disregard (para 22(4)-(6), up to 60 days) applies to the 183-day, 46-day, ties-table and 90-day-tie counts, but **not** to the 30-day home-presence tests, the 91-day accommodation period, the 61-day family tie, or the 21-day term-time test. If the 30 Hong Kong days are missed, illness or travel disruption will not excuse it.

### 5.9 The third automatic UK test — a larger hazard than the work tie

#### What it is, and why it exists

**It is the working person's equivalent of the 183-day rule: a way of being made UK resident by your job rather than by your day count.**

Parliament's target was someone whose working life is plainly based in the UK but who manages their days carefully — the person holding down a full-time UK job while flying out often enough to stay under the thresholds. The test says, in effect: *if you work full-time in the UK, you are UK resident, and counting days will not save you.*

#### Why it matters here, when Julian is not working

Two features make it dangerous in a way the other tests are not:

1. **It is a Stage 2 automatic test.** Meet it and you are UK resident **at any day count** — no ties analysis, no Table B, and **the Hong Kong flat is no defence**. It sits outside everything this document otherwise relies on.
2. **It looks at a rolling 365 days, not a tax year.** Most of the tests here are anchored to 6 April–5 April, so this one can reach **backwards** into a year already lived. *(It is not the only thing in the SRT that looks outside the year — the para 8 91-day window may run past the year end (§0), the family tie turns on Sophia's year-end residence (§3), and Case 8 split-year depends on the **following** year. But it is the only **automatic residence test** that can reach back into a closed year.)*

#### The practical consequence

**A job taken in early 2027 could make 2026/27 a resident year retrospectively** — after the year has closed, and after every day-count decision in this document has already been made. That is what distinguishes it from the 40-day work tie, which only costs headroom within a year you can still see.

> **So it is not a "later" problem.** It is the only automatic residence test where a future decision reaches back and changes a settled position.
>
> *One limit worth knowing:* para 9(1)(e) requires at least one UK workday of more than 3 hours to fall **within the tax year in question**. So a job starting on or after 6 April 2027 cannot touch 2026/27. **A UK job starting before then can.**

#### How it works

§3 dismisses it because Julian is not working, which is correct today. But the mechanics matter before any job is accepted:

- It runs over **any rolling 365-day period**, part of which falls in the tax year — so it can straddle tax years and start accruing before anyone is watching.
- It is **not defeated by the Hong Kong flat**, and **needs no ties**.
- "Work" includes self-employment and work done physically in the UK for an overseas employer (para 27(1): "Work is done where it is actually done, regardless of where the employment is held").

**The sharpest point: the 365-day period can extend BEYOND 5 April 2027 and retrospectively determine 2026/27 status.** A normal full-time UK job starting now and continuing into 2027/28 could make 2026/27 a resident year *after the fact*, even if UK days stay under 183. The exposure is foreseeable on the day the job is accepted — but not after, so it must be modelled before signing.

If Julian starts any work, this — not the 40-day work tie — is the bigger exposure, and it must be assessed **before** the first day of work, not after.

Note also that **exceptional circumstances cannot be applied to any element of this test** — not the sufficient-hours calculation, the 75% threshold, the 31-day significant break, or the one-day-in-year requirement ([RFIG22230](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig22230)).

### 5.10 THE LONDON PROPERTY BETWEEN TENANCIES — does an unintended void create a risk?

*Added 24 Sep 2026 at Julian's request. **Short answer: no, for two independent reasons. No action is required.***

An earlier note warned that "an empty, available London flat is accommodation with a one-night threshold." That was written in the context of the shedding lever at §5.6 and overstated the risk when read on its own. Taken properly:

#### Reason 1 — a void does not make it a home again

The London flat stopped being Julian's home in 2013, **not because it is let, but because he moved out and set up home elsewhere**. Para 25(5): somewhere that was P's home "does not continue to count as such merely because P continues to hold an estate or interest in it **after P has moved out** … having set up home elsewhere."

A gap between tenants does not reverse that. He has not moved back in. HMRC's Jamal example is directly on point:

> *"Jamal purchases a house in the UK as an investment. Although the property is furnished it is currently standing empty, because he cannot find a suitable tenant. Jamal has never stayed at the property. The UK house is not his home."* ([RFIG22150](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig22150))

**So a void cannot bring the flat into the second automatic UK test.** It is not a UK home, and an empty property he does not use stays that way.

#### Reason 2 — even as available accommodation, it cannot add a tie

For a void to create an **accommodation tie**, two things must both be true (para 34(1)):

1. The flat is available to him for a **continuous 91 days** — plausible in a long void, and
2. **He spends at least one night there** — which, if he is in Hong Kong or at his mother's, he would not

**And even if both happened, it would change nothing**, because he already holds the accommodation tie from his mother's house, and **multiple available properties still produce only one tie** — para 31(4): *"each tie of P's must be of a different type"*, illustrated by:

> *"Although Julie has 2 places that count as available accommodation, she only has 1 accommodation tie."* ([RFIG22170](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig22170), Example 7)

Since Julian has confirmed the accommodation tie at his mother's is **permanent** (§5.6), a London void is absorbed by a tie he already has. It cannot produce a second one.

#### Where a genuine risk would arise

Not from a void, but from a change in use:

- **If he ever moved back into the London flat and lived there**, it would become his home — and a UK home is limb 1 of the second automatic UK test (§3). That is a different situation from an empty flat between tenants.
- **If he stayed nights there during a long void in a year when he had no other UK accommodation tie** — which cannot happen while the tie at his mother's stands.

#### Conclusion

> **An unintended void between tenancies carries no residence consequence for Julian.** He does not need to manage the tenancy schedule, avoid gaps, or rush a re-letting for tax reasons.
>
> **What does still matter about the London flat** is the opposite case: that it remains **commercially let with exclusive possession**, which is what keeps it out of the analysis entirely (§9.11). A void is harmless; a change of use is not.

## 6. IF EITHER BECOMES RESIDENT — the reliefs that do and do not apply

*These are not risks to avoid; they are the two reliefs that determine how much a resident year would actually cost. **Split-year treatment is not available to Julian. The 4-year FIG regime is.***

### 6.1 SPLIT-YEAR TREATMENT — why it matters, and why Julian does not qualify

**Why it matters.** Split-year treatment is the main damage-limitation mechanism in the SRT. Where a Case applies, the tax year is divided into a **UK part** and an **overseas part**, and the individual is taxed as a resident only for the UK part. It can turn a ruinous accidental resident year into a survivable one — particularly for someone who spent the first months of the year living and earning abroad.

**It is not elective.** [RFIG21010](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig21010): "The individual does not have a choice." If a Case applies, the year is split; if none applies, it is not.

#### Julian does not qualify — both relevant Cases fail

| Case | Requirement | Julian |
|---|---|---|
| **Case 4** — starting to have a home in the UK **only** | Must **cease to have any overseas home** | ❌ **Fails.** He retains the Hong Kong flat — which is deliberate, since that flat is what defeats the second automatic UK test (§5.8) |
| **Case 8** — starting to have a home in the UK | (i) No UK home at the start of the year (para 51(3)) — ✅ **satisfied**, the London flat is let (§9.11) and he had no UK home on 6 Apr 2026; **(ii) UK resident in the FOLLOWING tax year, and that year not itself split (para 51(5))** | ❌ **Fails on (ii).** On the Hong Kong plan he is non-resident in 2027/28 |

> **Note the bind in Case 4.** The Hong Kong flat is simultaneously his best protection (it defeats the second automatic UK test) and the thing that blocks Case 4. He cannot have both. Given the flat delivers automatic non-residence while split-year only limits damage after residence has already happened, **keeping the flat is clearly the right trade** — but it should be a conscious one.

#### The consequence

**Any accidental resident year would be a FULL year from 6 April — with no overseas part.**

That means a resident 2026/27 would sweep in the period **6 April to 30 July 2026**, when Julian was living entirely in Hong Kong, as well as everything since.

> ### ⚠️ This applies to 2026/27 only — do not generalise it
> *(Corrected 24 Sep 2026.)* Cases 4 and 8 are **arrival** cases, and both fail for 2026/27 for the reasons above. **But the departure Cases 1–3 require UK residence in the previous tax year, so they are available precisely to someone in the leaver ratchet (§5.4).** If Julian were resident in 2026/27 and then began full-time Hong Kong work, **Case 1 could split 2027/28**. The conclusion "no split-year relief" is specific to the arrival year, not to every year.

**How much that costs depends on the year:**

- **2026/27 — near-harmless.** No employment income, so only the London rental is affected (the “What is at stake” section in Part One).
- **An earning year — material.** A full year of Hong Kong salary would fall into UK scope from 6 April, rather than only from an arrival date. This is one of the clearest reasons to avoid tipping into residence in a year with income, and a reason the leaver ratchet at §5.4 compounds: each of the three ratchet years is a year in which an accidental residence would be unsplit.

**Case 8 could become available** if plans changed such that Julian were UK resident in the following tax year too. Worth re-testing if the plan changes.

> ### ⚠️ Three qualifications on the departure Cases (added 24 Sep 2026)
> 1. **Cases 1–3 require residence in the *immediately preceding* tax year — not merely being inside the three-year leaver lookback.** Residence in 2026/27 followed by non-residence in 2027/28 leaves Julian a leaver in 2028/29, but the preceding-year condition then fails, so no departure Case is available that year.

> 3. **Cases 4 and 5 must be reconsidered if residence arises by a different route.** The analysis above excludes Case 4 because the Hong Kong home is retained — but the scenario this document most warns about is residence caused by *losing* that home, in which case Case 4 becomes live again. Likewise **Case 5** (starting full-time UK work) would need considering if residence arose through work. Neither necessarily succeeds; neither can be excluded on the original facts.

### 6.2 THE 4-YEAR FIG REGIME — the relief waiting if either becomes resident

*Summary only. The fuller analysis of the tax on letting the Hong Kong flat as a UK resident, with and without a FIG claim, is now in [[uktax-HKrental-income]] (24 Sep 2026).*

#### What it is

From **6 April 2025** the old non-domicile remittance basis was abolished and replaced by the **Foreign Income and Gains (FIG) regime**. It lets someone arriving in the UK after a long period abroad claim relief on their **foreign** income and gains for the **first resident year and the three tax years following it**.

#### Who qualifies — a "qualifying new resident"

Four conditions, all required ([RFIG44000](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig44000), s845B ITTOIA 2005):

- UK resident in the tax year
- **Not UK resident for at least 10 consecutive tax years immediately before it**
- At least 10 years old at the start of the tax year
- Not a member of the Commons or Lords

**Julian qualifies.** Continuously non-resident since 2013 gives 12 consecutive years (2014/15–2025/26 even if the departure year was split, which counts as a resident year for this purpose).

**Sophia qualifies too** — 12 non-resident years, and she is over 10.

#### What it covers, and what it does not

| Covered | Not covered |
|---|---|
| Foreign income — overseas rental, dividends, interest, foreign pensions | **UK-source income of any kind** |
| Foreign chargeable gains | The London rental — UK property, taxable either way |
| Overseas Workday Relief on employment duties performed abroad | Gains on UK land |

> ⚠️ **Do not assume FIG relief shelters a Hong Kong salary.** Employment income relief runs through **Overseas Workday Relief**, which has separate rules and a cap, not through the general FIG claim. Treat the two as distinct.
>
> **The Hong Kong property is the relevant one here.** If Julian became UK resident and let it, the rental income would be **foreign** income — within scope of the relief for four years. The London rental would not be.

#### The catch: the clock runs whether or not you use it

**The four years start with your first year of UK residence and run regardless of whether you claim.** HMRC's own example makes the point: an individual resident from 2025-26 "would not be a qualifying new resident in 2029-30" whether or not he claimed anything in between.

> ⚠️ **This is why an accidental resident year is expensive even in a year with no income.** Becoming resident in 2026/27 — with no employment income and the Hong Kong flat unlet — would consume **year one of four** and shelter nothing. The relief would then be three years old by the time there was anything to shelter.

You need not be resident continuously: if you are non-resident in year two you simply cannot claim that year, and can resume in year three if resident again. But the four-year window itself does not pause.

#### Claiming has a price

Claiming the relief in a year generally costs the **personal allowance** and the **capital gains annual exempt amount** for that year, so it is a calculation rather than an automatic win — worth it on large foreign income, not on small. **Overseas Workday Relief is also capped** under the post-2025 rules (broadly the lower of 30% of qualifying earnings or £300,000), unlike the older uncapped version. **Both points need an adviser's numbers; treat them as flags, not figures.**

#### Why it matters for the decisions in this document

1. **It lowers the cost of becoming resident** — but only for *foreign* income, and only for four years.
2. **It makes drifting into residence worse than choosing it.** The clock is a wasting asset; spending a year of it accidentally, in a year with nothing to shelter, is pure loss.
3. **Sophia has her own four-year window**, and her 28 January 2027 line would start it at age 12 — spent entirely before she has any income (§6).

*Standalone reference section. Sophia is not boarding for the Sept 2026 or Sept 2027 academic years. Sept 2028 is the earliest realistic start.*

### 7.1 Which tax years are affected

|---|---|---|

| Sept 2027 – Jul 2028 | ❌ No (realistically) | 2027/28, 2028/29 |

**The first tax year in which boarding could bite is 2028/29.** A 90-day tie created in 2026/27 reaches only 2027/28 and 2028/29, so **the 2026/27 decision and the boarding years overlap in one partial year** — and even there the tie may arise from 2027/28 instead.

### 7.2 Will Sophia be UK resident once boarding?

Probably yes, and it is hard to avoid. A UK academic year from September to early April is roughly 180–215 days in the UK depending on where holidays are spent.

- **183+ UK days → automatically resident** (first automatic UK test), no ties analysis.
- Below 183, she has at most **2 ties** — accommodation and 90-day. Family and work ties remain structurally impossible for her (§4).
- ⚠️ **But the 90-day tie is not automatic — and the 2026/27 decision controls it.** Her 2028/29 90-day tie exists only if she exceeded 90 UK days in 2026/27 *or* 2027/28. **If she is held at ≤90 in both, she has one tie in 2028/29 and is non-resident up to 182 days.** So Sophia's 27 October 2026 line does reach her own boarding-year status.
- ⚠️ **2028/29 is genuinely borderline on 183.** Term-time alone from a 4 September start to 5 April is ~183 midnights. If both Christmas and Easter are spent in Hong Kong she could fall *under* 183. From 2029/30 (three full terms, ~264 days) she is certainly resident.
- **If she becomes resident in 2026/27** (183 days on 28 Jan 2027), she is a "leaver" for 2027/28–2029/30: **Table A** applies, the **country tie** becomes available and would bite in a boarding year, and accommodation + country = 2 ties makes her resident above 90 days.
- She is also exposed to the **second automatic UK test** unless she achieves **30+ days in the Hong Kong flat** each year and it remains her home (§5.8). **Once boarding, this gets materially harder.**

**Plan on Sophia being UK resident in every boarding year.** The question is not how to prevent it, but how to stop it reaching Julian.

> **Note: school boarding accommodation is itself an accommodation tie for Sophia, and cannot be shed.** Para 34(3)(c) covers accommodation "otherwise available to P where P can live when P is in the UK" and para 34(4) requires no legal right to occupy. An autumn term of ~100 days is a continuous 91-day availability period on its own, half-terms of under 16 days are bridged by para 34(2), and she sleeps there. Her accommodation tie therefore does not depend on her grandmother's house.

### 7.3 How it reaches Julian — and the two ways to block it

Sophia being UK resident gives Julian a **family tie** — but only if he also sees her **in the UK** on 61+ days.

**Route 1 — para 33 education carve-out.** All five required:
1. Sophia is UK resident
2. Under 18
3. In full-time education in the UK at any time in the tax year
4. **Would not be UK resident** if her UK education time were disregarded
5. Spends **fewer than 21 days (max 20)** in the UK outside term-time

Term-time **includes half-terms and inset days** (para 33(6)), so half-term in the UK is free. **Christmas, Easter and summer count against the 20 days.** Exceptional circumstances cannot be deducted ([RFIG22230](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig22230)). HMRC's Yok Lin example works exactly this way: UK boarding school, half-terms in the UK, main holidays in Thailand, 14 days outside term-time → no family tie for her parents.

Condition 4 is a fact-heavy counterfactual and condition 5 is demanding. **Treat Route 1 as a bonus, not a plan.**

**Route 2 — the 61-day rule. This is the one to plan around.** Julian has no family tie unless he sees Sophia in person **in the UK** on 61+ days (para 32(3)). **Days spent with her wholly outside the UK do not count at all** ([RFIG22330](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/residence-and-fig-regime-manual/rfig22330); the Pierre example — 70 days with his daughter, 35 in Paris and Spain, 35 in the UK → no family tie).

> ⚠️ Para 32(4): seeing her for **"all or part of the day"** counts. A day that begins together in the UK and ends on a flight to Hong Kong is still a UK contact day.

Route 2 depends only on Julian's own diary — not on Sophia's school calendar, her residence status, or a counterfactual test.

### 7.4 The self-defeating holiday pattern

*Boundary case, not Julian's stated plan — see §7.8 for the actual intention, which is far lighter.*

A full UK school-holiday year is roughly **100 days**: summer ~45, Christmas ~17, Easter ~17, three half-terms ~21. *(Independent boarding schools are typically longer — 8–9 weeks summer, 3–4 weeks each at Christmas and Easter, plus half-terms and exeats, nearer **110–130 days**. The 100-day figure is conservative, which strengthens the conclusion below.)*

Spent **in the UK, at his mother's**, that single pattern triggers everything at once:

| Tie | Triggered by the same 100 days |
|---|---|
| **Accommodation** | 16+ nights at his mother's — one summer does it |
| **Family** | 61+ UK contact days with Sophia |
| **90-day** | The 100 days itself, biting in the *following* two years |

And it compounds on itself:

- Year N: ~100 UK days
- Year N+1: accommodation + family + 90-day = **3 ties → ceiling 90 days**
- Same pattern repeats → **UK RESIDENT**

**This is self-defeating regardless of anything decided in 2026/27.** It is a structural feature of the boarding plan, not a consequence of this autumn.

### 7.5 The sustainable pattern

**≤90 UK days per year, every year, from 2028/29 onward.** Exceeding 90 in *any* year creates a tie biting the next two, so the ceiling ratchets down exactly when it is needed most.

| Julian's habit in boarding years | Ties | Ceiling | Sustainable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~100 UK days, holidays in the UK | 3 | 90 | ❌ **Resident** |
| ≤90 UK days, holidays in the UK | accommodation + family = 2 | 120 | ✅ Yes, with margin |
| ≤90 UK days, **holidays in Hong Kong** | accommodation = 1 | 182 | ✅ **Comfortable** |

> **Why ≤90 is the robust discipline:** Table B requires **all 4 ties** in the 46–90 band. Julian can never hold 4 (the work tie needs 40 UK workdays, which the plan excludes). **So at 90 UK days or fewer he is non-resident at any tie count he can realistically reach** — the discipline is safe even if the tie position deteriorates. Row 2 above is sustainable only if *every* year is ≤90; a single year above it adds the third tie to the next two.

### 7.6 The single highest-leverage habit

**Bring Sophia to Hong Kong for her holidays rather than travelling to the UK.**

One change defeats three ties simultaneously:
- Contact days outside the UK do not count toward the **61** → no family tie
- They are not **UK days** → no 90-day tie, and the ≤90 habit becomes easy
- They are not **nights at his mother's** → the accommodation tie may even become shed-able (§5.6)

It also helps Sophia: time in Hong Kong supports the Hong Kong flat remaining **her** home (§5.8), which is her weakest point once she is in UK school.

**Half-terms are the exception worth using.** If Route 1 is ever in play, half-terms count as term-time, so a half-term visit to the UK is free under para 33 — though it still counts as a UK day and a contact day for Julian.

### 7.7 Checklist for the boarding years

1. **≤90 UK days per year** for Julian — the hard discipline
2. **<61 UK contact days** with Sophia — the family-tie block
3. **Holidays in Hong Kong** wherever possible — serves both of the above
4. **30+ days in a Hong Kong home** for *both*, every year — in whichever property is currently their home. Letting the owned flat is fine **provided another Hong Kong home replaces it immediately**; a gap with no overseas home is what breaks the defence (§5.8)
5. **No work physically done in the UK** — 40 days creates a work tie; a remote HK job worked from the UK also risks the third automatic UK test (§5.9)

## 10. Principles captured

Durable rules extracted from this analysis.

1. **The waterfall governs.** Automatic overseas beats automatic UK beats ties. Under 46 days, nothing else can reach you.
2. **Defeating a test at one stage does not help at another.** It only moves you down the waterfall. *(See §2 — the central trap.)*
3. **"Home" and "accommodation" are different concepts in different tests.** The overseas home is an escape hatch at Stage 2 and irrelevant at Stage 3.
4. **The 30-day symmetry runs in opposite directions.** 30+ days in the **UK** home moves the automatic test *toward* catching you; 30+ days in the **overseas** home *stops* it. Same number, opposite effect.
5. **Ties are connection markers, not tests.** They have no overseas offset. Having a home abroad does not weaken a UK connection.
6. **The first tie is free; the second one costs.** No Table B band is triggered by one tie. The accommodation tie is inert alone and dangerous paired.
7. **A day is presence at midnight.** Travel days are decided by the clock, not the calendar. Recollected day counts drift upward when counted properly.
8. **The 90-day tie reaches forward two years.** This year's count constrains the next two.
9. **90 is the forward threshold; 121 is the current-year threshold.** Crossing 121 costs nothing further for future years.
10. **Sophia's status is the control variable.** The family tie runs downward only, so her residence is determined independently and then feeds into Julian's. She cannot be dragged in by him; he can be dragged in by her.
11. **The 30 days in Hong Kong is an annual obligation.** No carry-over. Miss it in any single year and the automatic test catches that person that year.
12. **Cliffs, not slopes.** 29 days in the HK flat is worth exactly nothing. 182 days is safe at one tie; 183 is automatic residence. There is no proportionality anywhere in the SRT. *(One qualification: the exceptional-circumstances disregard, up to 60 days, softens the 183-day, 46-day, ties-table and 90-day-tie counts — but never the 30-day home tests, the 91-day accommodation period, the 61-day family tie, or the 21-day term-time test.)*
13. **Arriver status is an asset.** Losing it triggers a three-year ratchet: Table A, the country tie, loss of the under-46-day escape, and the deeming rule.
14. **"Available" is not "occupied."** Accommodation counts as available if it is at your disposal, whether or not you are in it.
15. **Structure protects; intention does not.** An overseas home only works if genuinely used for 30 days each year. A retained asset is not a defence until the days are actually spent.
16. **Necessary is not sufficient.** The 30 days satisfies para 8(5), but the property must *also* remain a "home" throughout (para 25), and there must be no 91-day window in which a UK home exists and no overseas home does. Meeting one limb of a multi-limb test proves nothing about the others.
17. **An assumption marked "confirmed" is the one that fails.** The original draft recorded the Hong Kong flat's home status as settled. Two independent reviews identified it as the single point of failure. Fact-sensitive characterisations are never settled — HMRC may enquire into them and reach its own view.

18. **The protection is the category, not the asset.** What defeats the second automatic UK test is having *an* overseas home you actually use — not owning a particular flat. Ownership is irrelevant (para 25(4)); a rented place you live in counts. So the owned flat can be let or sold without harm **provided another home immediately takes over**. What is fatal is a *gap* in which no overseas home exists at all. The obligation follows wherever you currently live; it does not stay with the property, and history in a former home does not satisfy it.

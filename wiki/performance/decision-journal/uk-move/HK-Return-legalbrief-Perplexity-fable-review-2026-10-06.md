# Fable Review: HK Return Legal Position (6 Oct 2026)

It exists so the specialist lawyer is briefed on where the two sources are right, wrong, or silent, before any decision to travel is made.

## Findings

### A. Criminal offence (Child Abduction Act 1984, s1)

**A1. Is it an offence? CONFIRMED, serious, report correct.**
- Claim tested: taking Sophia out of the UK without Clodagh's consent or a court order is an offence under s1.
- Finding: correct. s1(1): "a person connected with a child under the age of sixteen commits an offence if he takes or sends the child out of the United Kingdom without the appropriate consent." Julian is "connected" as a parent (s1(2)(a)). "Appropriate consent" (s1(3)) is the consent of each of: the child's mother (s1(3)(a)(i)), the father if he has parental responsibility, any guardian, special guardian, UK "lives with" order holder or UK custody holder; or the leave of the court under Part II Children Act 1989 (s1(3)(b)); or, where someone has "custody", the leave of the court that awarded it (s1(3)(c)). Clodagh's consent is required by name as the mother. Nothing in s1 turns on habitual residence or on whether the UK stay was temporary.
- Evidence: legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1984/37/section/1 (current version). Reunite's own Prevention Guide for England and Wales (2020), p1: "It is a criminal offence to take a child out of the UK without the consent of everybody with parental responsibility, unless the court has given permission."
- What it changes: the Reunite adviser's "should be able to move without Clodagh's permission" cannot stand as a statement of English criminal law. Reunite's written definition of abduction ("outside of their country of habitual residence") is the civil Hague framing; the adviser appears to have answered the Hague question, not the criminal one. Both sources can be right only in that split sense.

**A2. s1(4) and (4A) exceptions. CONFIRMED, minor, report correct.**
- The one-month exception is for a person named in a UK child arrangements order as the person the child lives with; three months for a special guardian. Neither exists here. s1(4A) removes even those where a Part II order is breached.

**A3. s1(5) defences and s1(6) burden. CONFIRMED on text, PLAUSIBLE on application, serious: report incomplete in Julian's favour and against him.**
- Claim tested: the s1(5) defences "should not be treated as a safe route".
- Finding: right conclusion, wrong reasoning. The report misses that s1(5)(c) (other parent "has unreasonably refused to consent") IS available to Julian: s1(5A) removes it only where the refuser holds a UK "lives with" order, UK custody or special guardianship, or where the taking breaches "an order made by a court in the United Kingdom". The 2020 Hong Kong order is not a UK order (s1(7)(b)). And s1(6) puts the burden on the prosecution to disprove the defence once evidence raises it. Two limits: (i) "unreasonably refused" presupposes a refusal: Julian must make a proper, documented proposal and be refused; the current plan (tell her just before departure) gives her nothing to refuse, and s1(5)(a)(ii) ("would consent if aware of all the relevant circumstances") is not credible where secrecy is chosen because objection is expected. (ii) The defence disappears the moment Clodagh obtains a prohibited steps order (s1(5A)(b)).
- What it changes: a transparent proposal first is not just "advisable", it is the only way any s1(5) defence can exist. Secrecy forfeits the defences the Act gives him.

**A4. Does the Hong Kong order count as a "relevant order" for s1? CONFIRMED on text, PLAUSIBLE on construction, serious, not addressed by the report.**
- s1(7)(b): a person has "custody" only under "an order of a court in the United Kingdom". So the HK order does not make Clodagh a custody-holder for s1(3)(a)(v) or s1(5A), and Hong Kong court leave does not appear to satisfy s1(3)(c) (which refers to leave of the court that awarded "custody" as so defined). No case law checked on whether a foreign order could be read in.
- What it changes: for the English criminal exposure, only Clodagh's consent or an English Part II order (specific issue order granting permission) is a clean answer. A Hong Kong variation or leave alone may not be. This bears directly on forum choice.

**A5. Penalty, prosecution gate, realistic risk. CONFIRMED on statute, PLAUSIBLE on risk, serious.**
- s4: on indictment up to seven years; summary up to six months or fine. s4(2): no prosecution without the DPP's consent. CPS treats s1 within its child abuse (non-sexual) guidance; public-interest factors specific to a parent with no UK order were not retrievable (PLAUSIBLE: such cases are rarely charged, but the Act contains no such carve-out).
- The UK suspended its extradition treaty with Hong Kong on 20 July 2020 "immediately and indefinitely"; Hong Kong reciprocated. No extradition from Hong Kong, but any warrant bites the moment Julian re-enters the UK (visiting his mother) and may bite in transit states. A six-year plan with a UK-based grandmother makes that exposure real, not theoretical.

### B. Interpretation of the 2020 Hong Kong order

**B1. Custody versus care and control. CONFIRMED (secondary source), minor, report correct.**
- HK usage: custody = major decisions; care and control = day-to-day care and residence. Both are joint here (order para 1; Parental Agreement Parenting Plan para 1). CLIC (Community Legal Information Centre, HK) page as cited by the report.

**B2. Removal proviso. CONFIRMED on text, minor, report sound; one wording sensitivity.**
- Text: child "not be removed from Hong Kong without leave until she attains the age of 18 years but provided that if either parent do give a general written undertaking to the court to return the said child to Hong Kong when called upon to do so, and unless otherwise directed with the written consent of the other parent, that parent may remove the said child from Hong Kong for any period specified in such written consent."
- The report's reading (undertaking to the court plus the other parent's written consent) is right. Missed: the consent must specify a period; the 8 Sep 2026 letter is open-ended, so it does not fit the proviso. "Unless otherwise directed" preserves the court's override.
- Wording sensitivity for the scan check: the clause "and unless otherwise directed with the written consent" is grammatically broken. If the sealed text read "or" rather than "and" between the undertaking and the consent limb, an undertaking alone might have sufficed. Worth confirming against the scan.

**B3. Exclusive jurisdiction clause. CONFIRMED on text, PLAUSIBLE on effect, minor, report correct.**
- It sits in the Parental Agreement's General Provisions and binds the parents as to "this Agreement" and applications under it. A parental forum clause cannot oust an English court's welfare jurisdiction over a child present or habitually resident in England; it is a factor in forum and stay decisions (FLA 1986 s5) and in the HK court's view of itself as the continuing forum.

**B4. Effect of changed practice. CONFIRMED on text, minor, report correct and understated.**
- Practice does not vary the order. Add: the Agreement's Effectiveness clause requires any modification to be "in writing and signed with the same formality as the original Agreement", so WhatsApp exchanges and the unfiled letter are not modifications; and the DBIS "year 2 to year 13" clause (Parenting Plan paras 2-3) was itself departed from in September 2026 by both parents, by written consent of a kind that may not meet that formality.

### C. Impact of the order on the UK-to-HK move

**C1. Was the July 2026 departure compliant? CONFIRMED on text, PLAUSIBLE on consequences, serious: report understates.**
- No leave was obtained; no undertaking to return was given; the consent was not period-specific. The direction was breached, by both parents acting together. The retrospective letter apologises and (deliberately, on the HK lawyer's advice) gives no undertaking.
- Consequences: the breach is symmetrical, so Clodagh cannot use it cleanly against Julian, but the HK court remains seised and may ask for the undertaking when the letter is filed. If Julian later relies on the HK court as his forum, he arrives as a party in breach of its direction. The report's "technical complication" is the right direction, too soft a label.
- Record inconsistency to fix: [[HK-Return-legalbrief-Perplexity]] dates Sophia's arrival in England at 30 July 2026; [[court-order-care-and-control]] and [[removal-notification-letter]] say "Sept 2026". The letter file's status line still says "DRAFT, not signed"; the report calls it "signed but unfiled".

**C2. Does the order bear on the UK-to-HK move as the report says? CONFIRMED on text, PLAUSIBLE on court weight, serious: report one-sided.**
- In England the consent requirement does not come from the HK order. It comes from Clodagh's parental responsibility as the mother under English law and s1 CAA 1984. The order adds nothing to that and (A4) subtracts nothing.
- The order's operative direction is non-removal FROM Hong Kong until 18. Returning Sophia TO Hong Kong does not breach it; the order and Agreement treat Hong Kong as her home and DBIS as her school to Year 13. The report lists the order only under "factors supporting England" ("contemplated joint decisions and a shared schedule"). It belongs on both sides, and in Hong Kong it leans Julian's way.

**C3. Help or hurt, by forum. PLAUSIBLE, serious.**
- Hong Kong: continuing seisin; a variation or relocation application there would start from the order; on a Hague return application the order cannot itself be a ground to refuse (Art 17, see D3) though its reasons may be weighed.
- England: the order proves Clodagh's joint custody and that the UK move was itself a departure from the agreed plan. The Agreement's mediation-first clause (para 22: up to five hours of mediation "prior to filing any motion to modify this parenting plan") helps Julian if he offers mediation first and hurts him if he bypasses it; it also gives Clodagh an argument that a Hague application is not a "motion to modify" and so is not caught.

**C4. Immigration stop note. CONFIRMED on text, minor, not in report.**
- The order's note: "Either parent may serve this Order on the Director of Immigration to restrain the child from being removed from Hong Kong." Once Sophia is back in Hong Kong, Clodagh can use this to stop her leaving again, which would hit any contact plan built on Sophia flying to Ireland or the UK.

### D. What the report misses

**D1. English jurisdiction exists on presence alone. CONFIRMED on statute, serious: Reunite wrong, report too gentle.**
- FLA 1986 s2(1)(b)(ii) with s3(1)(b): where the 1996 Hague Convention does not apply, an English court may make a s8 order (prohibited steps, specific issue) if the child "is present in England and Wales and is not habitually resident in any part of the United Kingdom". Hong Kong is not a 1996 Convention state, so if Sophia's habitual residence is still Hong Kong, presence suffices; if it has shifted to England, jurisdiction arises under the Convention (Art 5) anyway. Either way, while she is in England, Clodagh can obtain a prohibited steps order. The court may stay on forum grounds (s5), but that is discretionary and slow, and an interim order would stand meanwhile. Case-law nuance: the relevant date is the date of application, so she must apply while Sophia is here (Re S (A Child) (Jurisdiction) [2022] EWHC 1720 (Fam), where jurisdiction was lost once the child had left).
- What it changes: "UK unlikely to hold jurisdiction" is wrong as to pre-departure orders. The report's "jurisdiction is not automatic" is true only of the merits question.

**D2. Habitual residence can shift within months despite a temporary intention. CONFIRMED, fatal to the Reunite premise.**
- AR v RN [2015] UKSC 35: children moved to Scotland in July 2013 for a planned stay of about a year; by 20 November 2013 (about four months) they were habitually resident in Scotland. "There is no requirement that ... one or both parents intend to reside there permanently or indefinitely"; "an intention to live in a country for a limited period [is not] inconsistent with becoming habitually resident there"; "the important question is whether the residence has the necessary quality of stability, not whether it is necessarily intended to be permanent"; "there is no rule that one parent cannot unilaterally change the habitual residence of a child."
- Sophia: arrived 30 July, school from 5 September, living with father and grandmother under an indefinite "Plan B". By a December departure she will have been in England about four and a half months, longer than the children in AR v RN. The retained Hong Kong home and the conditional return intention are relevant but, on this authority, not decisive.
- What it changes: there is a material, possibly better-than-even, risk that Sophia is habitually resident in England at the date of any removal. That is the hinge of a Hague claim.

**D3. The Hague return application in Hong Kong. CONFIRMED on Convention text and HK framework, PLAUSIBLE on outcome, fatal to "probably not succeed".**
- Framework: Hong Kong applies the 1980 Convention through the Child Abduction and Custody Ordinance (Cap 512), in force 5 September 1997; the Convention operates between Hong Kong and the UK from 1 September 1997; Central Authority is the Secretary for Justice; the Court of First Instance has exclusive jurisdiction. Clodagh can apply from Ireland (Art 8 allows application to any Central Authority).
- Art 3: removal is wrongful if in breach of rights of custody under the law of the state of habitual residence (England, if D2 goes against Julian) that were actually exercised or would have been. English law treats a parent with parental responsibility as holding rights of custody by virtue of the s1 CAA consent requirement, even with no court order (PLAUSIBLE: INCADAT and practitioner commentary; cases not read). "Actually exercising" is read liberally; Clodagh's Ireland base weakens but is unlikely to defeat it if she maintains contact.
- Art 12: within one year of removal the court "shall order the return of the child forthwith"; settlement in the new environment is a defence only where proceedings start more than a year after removal. Reunite's "once she is back in Hong Kong and in school it would probably not succeed" is an Art 12 second-limb argument that does not exist inside the year.
- Art 13(b) grave risk: the alcohol and safeguarding evidence would run here, but Hong Kong applies a demanding standard and prefers return with protective undertakings. Secretary for Justice v C and S [2024] HKCFI 2821: return to Germany ordered, a 9-year-old's objection given "little weight" as influenced, costs against the mother, and the court saying it "should send out a clear message to potential abductors that there are no safe havens among the contracting states". Reported returns in 2025 within 8-10 weeks of issue.
- Art 13 child's objection: Sophia is 12; an independently formed objection carries real weight, but it is discretionary, and the court will probe for influence. "She will do whatever I think is best" is the worst possible evidence on this limb.
- Art 17: the Hong Kong court's own 2020 custody order "shall not be a ground for refusing to return", though its reasons may be considered. Art 16: no merits decision in Hong Kong while the Hague case is pending.
- Consequence if return is ordered: Sophia back to England within weeks, the relocation merits then decided in England with an abduction finding against Julian, likely costs (Art 26), and the job lost anyway.

**D4. England keeps jurisdiction for a year after a non-consensual removal. CONFIRMED, serious.**
- FLA 1986 s41: a child under 16 habitually resident in England who becomes habitually resident elsewhere after removal "without the agreement of the person or all the persons having ... the right to determine where he is to reside" is treated as still habitually resident in England for one year. Clodagh could obtain English child arrangements or return orders during that year. They are not directly enforceable in Hong Kong, but they feed the Hague application and create contempt exposure in England.

**D5. Passports and port alerts. CONFIRMED (Reunite guide and practitioner sources), minor to serious.**
- Port alert: police can list a child under 16 for 28 days without a court order where the risk is "real and imminent" (24-48 hours); Clodagh can trigger it from Ireland by phone if she learns a date. After departure it is useless, hence the pressure on timing.
- Passports: with the father holding parental responsibility, HM Passport Office will note Clodagh's objection only on a UK court order; it cannot force surrender of an existing passport. Sophia is probably entitled to an Irish passport as well as a British one: the lawyer should know what exists and who holds it. A High Court passport surrender order is the realistic tool if she applies.

**D6. Merits damage is bigger than "may damage the merits". PLAUSIBLE, serious.**
- English and Hong Kong courts treat a unilateral international move as a serious welfare and credibility negative; it can lead to a transfer of primary care, not just a refusal of permission. Conversely, a documented offer of mediation (Reunite offers it, and the 2020 Agreement para 22 requires it before a motion to modify) both complies with the order and builds the refusal record that s1(5)(c) needs.

**D7. The age clock. CONFIRMED, minor.**
- Both s1 CAA 1984 and the Convention (Art 4) stop at 16: 25 May 2030 for Sophia. Not an escape now, but a six-year plan straddles that date; after it neither mechanism applies to her.

**D8. Financial figure. CONFIRMED, minor, report correct.**
- The sealed order records mutual undertakings of HK$12,500 per month each; the HK$8,500 figure in the factual account is unexplained. The report is right to flag it.

## Questions for the specialist lawyer

1. On these facts (arrived 30 July 2026, English school from 5 September, indefinite "Plan B", Hong Kong home retained), is Sophia habitually resident in England now, and will she be by early December? Which single piece of evidence moves it most, given AR v RN [2015] UKSC 35?
2. If Julian leaves without consent, is s1 Child Abduction Act 1984 engaged regardless of the habitual-residence answer, and is the s1(5)(c) "unreasonable refusal" defence realistically available if he first makes a documented proposal and is refused? How does the DPP consent gate play with no UK order in place?
3. Given s1(7)(b), does Hong Kong court leave count as "appropriate consent" under s1(3)(c), or is only Clodagh's written consent or an English Part II order a clean answer?
4. If Clodagh applies to the Hong Kong Central Authority within the year: how would the Court of First Instance approach Art 3 (her rights of custody and "actual exercise" from Ireland), Art 13(b) on the alcohol and safeguarding record, and a 12-year-old's objection? Does the 2020 Hong Kong order carry any weight given Art 17?
5. Forum and sequence against the 10 December deadline: specific issue order in England, variation or leave in Hong Kong under the exclusive-jurisdiction clause, or both with mirror orders? Which is fastest, and which protects against the criminal exposure in Q3?
6. Was the July 2026 departure a breach of the 2020 direction (no leave, no undertaking, open-ended consent)? Should the retrospective letter be filed as drafted, and what happens if the court asks for the undertaking to return?
7. Does the Parental Agreement's mediation-first clause (para 22) bind either parent before litigating, and can Reunite mediation satisfy it while also building the s1(5)(c) record?
8. If he goes anyway: what is the practical exposure on re-entering the UK, what could an English court order under FLA 1986 s41 within the year, and what are the port-alert and passport (British and Irish) risks before departure?

# Return to Hong Kong with Sophia: Legal Brief

## Purpose

The reviewed legal position on whether Julian can take Sophia (12) from England back to Hong Kong for about six years without Clodagh's consent, written as the brief he hands a specialist international-children lawyer in England and Wales and in Hong Kong. It replaces [[HK-Return-legalbrief-Perplexity]] as the working document; that report, its adversarial review [[HK-Return-legalbrief-Perplexity-fable-review-2026-10-06]] and its citation check [[HK-Return-legalbrief-Perplexity-sonnet-review-2026-10-06]] are kept for provenance.

> As of 6 Oct 2026: not reviewed by Codex or a lawyer. Statute and Convention text checked against legislation.gov.uk and hcch.net; Hong Kong court practice and English case law on rights of custody checked only through secondary reports. Every claim below is cited or labelled opinion.

## Bottom line

- Leaving the UK with Sophia without Clodagh's written consent or an English court's permission is, on the current text of s1 Child Abduction Act 1984, a criminal offence. Habitual residence and the temporary nature of the stay are irrelevant to the offence. Reunite's verbal "you should be able to go" contradicts Reunite's own written guide.
- An English court can make a prohibited steps order while Sophia is physically here, whether or not she is habitually resident here (Family Law Act 1986 s3(1)(b)). "The UK is unlikely to have jurisdiction" is wrong for pre-departure orders.
- Habitual residence has no day or month threshold. On UK Supreme Court authority a planned temporary stay of months can shift it. By Christmas Sophia will have had about four months of school and home life in England; a court could well find her habitually resident here. That is the hinge of a Hague return claim in Hong Kong.
- If Clodagh applied in Hong Kong within a year, there is no "settled" defence (Art 12) and the 2020 Hong Kong order cannot itself defeat return (Art 17). Hong Kong courts return quickly and strictly. A return order would bring Sophia back to England with an abduction finding against Julian.
- The 2020 Hong Kong order does not create the English consent problem (Clodagh's parental responsibility does) and does not forbid a return to Hong Kong; in Hong Kong it leans Julian's way. The lawful routes are Clodagh's informed written consent, ideally made an order, or a permission application, sequenced now against a Christmas departure.

## Criminal law (England and Wales)

| Point | Position | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Offence | s1(1): a "person connected with a child under the age of sixteen" (a parent, s1(2)(a)) "commits an offence if he takes or sends the child out of the United Kingdom without the appropriate consent" | CONFIRMED [1] |
| Appropriate consent | s1(3): consent of each of the mother, the father with parental responsibility, any guardian, special guardian, UK "lives with" order holder or UK custody holder; or leave of the court under Part II Children Act 1989; or leave of the court that awarded "custody". Clodagh's consent is required by name as the mother | CONFIRMED [1] |
| Habitual residence | No element of s1 turns on it, or on how long or why Sophia is in the UK | CONFIRMED [1] |
| Exceptions s1(4), (4A) | One month for a UK "lives with" order holder; three months for a special guardian; neither exists here | CONFIRMED [1] |
| Defences s1(5) | (a) belief the other parent has consented or would consent knowing all the circumstances; (b) all reasonable steps to communicate failed; (c) other parent "has unreasonably refused to consent". s1(6): once raised, the prosecution must disprove | CONFIRMED [1] |
| Limit s1(5A) | (c) is unavailable where the refuser holds a UK "lives with", custody or special guardianship order, or where the taking breaches "an order made by a court in the United Kingdom". The Hong Kong order is not such an order (s1(7)(b)) | CONFIRMED [1] |

Only Clodagh's consent or an English Part II order is a clean answer | CONFIRMED on text; construction PLAUSIBLE [1] |
| Penalty and gate | s4: up to seven years on indictment; six months summary; no prosecution without DPP consent | CONFIRMED [2] |
| Extradition | UK suspended its extradition treaty with Hong Kong on 20 July 2020, "immediately and indefinitely"; Hong Kong reciprocated. Exposure is on re-entry to the UK or in transit states | CONFIRMED (suspension) [3]; exposure is opinion |

What this means (opinion): the s1(5)(c) defence exists for Julian only if he makes a proper, documented proposal and Clodagh refuses it unreasonably. Telling her just before departure gives her nothing to refuse, and s1(5)(a)(ii) is not credible where secrecy is chosen because objection is expected. Any prohibited steps order she obtains removes the defence (s1(5A)(b)). Prosecutions of a parent with no UK order in place are believed rare (PLAUSIBLE; CPS public-interest practice not verified [4]), but the Act has no such carve-out.

Reunite's own Prevention Guide (2020) states: "It is a criminal offence to take a child out of the UK without the consent of everybody with parental responsibility, unless the court has given permission." [5] The adviser's verbal view can be right only as a statement about a civil Hague claim, not about the offence.

## Jurisdiction and habitual residence

**English jurisdiction to make orders.** Family Law Act 1986 s2(1)(b)(ii) with s3(1)(b): where the 1996 Hague Convention does not apply, the court may make a s8 order (prohibited steps, specific issue) if the child "is present in England and Wales and is not habitually resident in any part of the United Kingdom". CONFIRMED [6]. The 1996 Convention has no entry for China, Hong Kong or Macao in the HCCH status table (checked 6 Oct 2026), so it does not apply. CONFIRMED [7]. If Sophia is habitually resident in England the court has jurisdiction anyway. The relevant date is the date of the application, so Clodagh must apply while Sophia is present (Re S (A Child) (Jurisdiction) [2022] EWHC 1720 (Fam), where jurisdiction was lost once the child had left). CONFIRMED [8]. A stay on forum grounds (s5) is discretionary and slow (opinion).

**Habitual residence: the test.**

| Authority | Holding | Status |
|---|---|---|
| A v A [2013] UKSC 60 | Habitual residence is a question of fact; the place reflecting "some degree of integration by the child in a social and family environment" | CONFIRMED [9] |
| Re LC [2014] UKSC 1 | For an adolescent, the child's own state of mind during the residence, as to its nature and quality, is relevant | CONFIRMED [10] |
| AR v RN [2015] UKSC 35 | "Stability of residence, rather than its degree of permanence, is important"; no required period; an intention to stay for a limited period is "not inconsistent with becoming habitually resident"; one parent can change it unilaterally. Children in Scotland about four months into a planned one-year stay were habitually resident there | CONFIRMED [11] |
| Re B [2016] UKSC 4 | The "see-saw": as ties to one country weaken and ties to another strengthen, habitual residence moves; a child is highly unlikely to be left with none | CONFIRMED [12] |

**Fixed time markers that do exist** (none of them defines habitual residence): Hague Art 12 one year from removal before the "settled" defence arises [13]; FLA 1986 s41 one year of deemed continuing English habitual residence after a non-consensual removal [6]; age 16, when both s1 CAA 1984 and the Convention (Art 4) stop applying, 25 May 2030 for Sophia [1][13]. The UK tax rule that 183 days in a tax year makes a person UK resident is a Statutory Residence Test rule for tax and has nothing to do with a child's habitual residence [14].

**Applied (opinion, for the lawyer to test):** integration arguably began on 26 August (decision) or 4 September (school), giving roughly four months by Christmas, comparable to AR v RN. For England: school, home with father and grandmother, an indefinite fallback, documented pre-departure planning. For Hong Kong: return tickets, retained home, twelve years of life and school there, the job-contingent nature of the stay, Sophia's own sense of where home is (Re LC). The honest expectation is that a court could go either way, with a real risk it finds England.

## The 2020 Hong Kong order

**Meaning.** Custody (major decisions) and care and control (daily care and residence) are both joint [15, 16]. Operative direction: Sophia "not be removed from Hong Kong without leave until she attains the age of 18 years", with a proviso: a parent who gives "a general written undertaking to the court to return the said child to Hong Kong when called upon to do so" may, with "the written consent of the other parent", remove her "for any period specified in such written consent" [15]. The Parental Agreement is incorporated: equal time, DBIS to Year 13 (modifiable only by express written consent of both), travel abroad during parenting time with written consent (three months' notice for long holiday trips), up to five hours of mediation before "filing any motion to modify this parenting plan", modifications only "in writing and signed with the same formality as the original Agreement", Hong Kong law and exclusive jurisdiction of the Hong Kong courts over the Agreement [15]. A parental forum clause cannot oust an English court's welfare jurisdiction over a child present here; it bears on forum and stay (opinion).

**Compliance of the July departure.** The 30 July summer trip was a removal "for a period"; the proviso required an undertaking to the court, which was never given, and period-specific written consent. The retention from 26 August and the school withdrawal were by mutual consent but without leave, undertaking or a formally executed modification of the DBIS clause. Both parents acted together, so the breach is symmetrical; the 8 September letter apologises and deliberately offers no undertaking. CONFIRMED on text; consequences PLAUSIBLE. Transcription sensitivity: the proviso's "and unless otherwise directed with the written consent" is grammatically broken; if the sealed text read "or", an undertaking alone might have sufficed. The scans were checked on 6 Oct and the transcription matches [17].

**Effect on a return to Hong Kong.** The direction forbids removal from Hong Kong; returning Sophia to Hong Kong does not breach it, and the order and Agreement treat Hong Kong as home and DBIS as her school to Year 13. In Hong Kong this leans Julian's way (opinion). In England the order proves Clodagh's joint custody and that the UK move was itself a departure from the agreed plan; but the English consent requirement comes from her parental responsibility as mother under English law and s1 CAA 1984, not from the order. Under Hague Art 17 the Hong Kong court's own order "shall not be a ground for refusing to return" [13]. Two practical notes: the order lets either parent serve it on the Director of Immigration to stop Sophia leaving Hong Kong, which Clodagh could use against future trips to Ireland or the UK [15]; and the mediation-first clause helps Julian if he offers mediation first and hurts him if he bypasses it (opinion).

## Lawful routes

**Consent route (lowest risk).** Clodagh's informed written consent to a six-year relocation, disclosing home, school, job, dates, contact schedule with funded flights, passport arrangements, information sharing and dispute resolution; ideally converted into an order (English consent order and, the Hong Kong lawyer to confirm, a Hong Kong variation). Reunite offers mediation; the 2020 Agreement's mediation-first clause makes a mediation offer both compliant and, if refused, the start of a s1(5)(c) record (opinion).

**Contested route.** Apply rather than leave. England: C100 for a specific issue order authorising relocation and determining living, schooling and contact; MIAM exemption may apply for urgency or safeguarding [23]. Hong Kong: variation of the 2020 order or leave under its direction, and filing the 8 September letter with advice on the undertaking. Forum and sequence are for counsel: the English order is the one that cleanly removes criminal exposure (s1(3)(b)); the Hong Kong court is the continuing forum under the exclusive-jurisdiction clause.

**Sequencing against Christmas (opinion).** Instruct both lawyers this week; evidence pack within two weeks; proposal to Clodagh with a mediation offer and a response date in October; if refused or silent, issue in the agreed forum in early November with a request for expedition tied to the 4 January 2027 term start. A contested relocation hearing before Christmas is unlikely; the lawyer should say whether an interim or consent-based outcome is realistic, and what happens to the job if not.

## Questions for the lawyer

1. On these facts (30 Jul summer departure with return tickets, 26 Aug fallback, school from 4 Sep, Hong Kong home retained, pre-departure UK planning in the record), is Sophia habitually resident in England now, and will she be by Christmas? Which single piece of evidence moves it most, given AR v RN?
2. If Julian leaves without consent, is s1 CAA 1984 engaged regardless of habitual residence, and is the s1(5)(c) defence realistically available if he first makes a documented proposal and is refused? How does the DPP consent gate play with no UK order?
3. Given s1(7)(b), does Hong Kong court leave count as "appropriate consent" under s1(3)(c), or is only Clodagh's written consent or an English Part II order a clean answer?
4. If Clodagh applies in Hong Kong within the year: how would the Court of First Instance approach Art 3 (her rights of custody and actual exercise from Ireland), Art 13(b) on the alcohol and safeguarding record, and a 12-year-old's objection? Does the 2020 order carry weight given Art 17?
5. Forum and sequence against a Christmas departure: specific issue order in England, variation or leave in Hong Kong under the exclusive-jurisdiction clause, or both with mirror orders? Which is fastest, and which removes the criminal exposure?
6. Was the July departure and August retention a breach of the 2020 direction (no leave, no undertaking, no period-specific consent)? Should the 8 September letter be filed as drafted, and what if the court asks for the undertaking?
7. Does the Parental Agreement's mediation-first clause bind either parent before litigating, does it catch a Hague application, and can Reunite mediation satisfy it?
8. If he goes anyway: exposure on re-entering the UK, what an English court could order under s41 within the year, and the port-alert and passport (British and Irish) risks before departure.

## Key Takeaways

- The criminal offence turns on consent at the UK border, not on habitual residence; Reunite's written guide agrees with this brief, the adviser's verbal view does not.
- An English court can stop departure while Sophia is here, on presence alone.
- Habitual residence has no clock; four months of school and home life is enough on existing authority, so a Hague return from Hong Kong within the year is a live risk and the Hong Kong order would not block it.
- The 2020 Hong Kong order is not the obstacle; in Hong Kong it helps. Clodagh's parental responsibility is the obstacle.
- Routes that work: her informed written consent made an order, or a permission application issued in early November with expedition sought for a 4 January 2027 start.

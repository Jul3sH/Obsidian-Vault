# MM: Assertiveness Is Professional

This is the Assertiveness Is Professional mental model: pushing back, negotiating,
and saying no are professional acts, not rudeness or making a fuss. It exists
because the lessons-learnt archive shows the opposite instinct recurring for
years, absorbing unfair asks, staying quiet on gaps, and reading pushback as
risky, when the actual cost fell on being seen as a walkover. Read it before
staying silent to keep the peace or look easy to work with.

**One-liner:** Pushing back is professional; being a walkover costs the respect
it was meant to buy.

**Reach for it when:** you're tempted to absorb an unfair ask, stay quiet on a
gap, or avoid negotiating because it feels like making a fuss.

## Key Takeaways

- A walkover is not read as helpful or efficient, it is read as weak; only do
  favours that are calculated, not reflexive.
- Negotiating, saying no, and asking questions are assertive rights, not
  impositions; the worst response is a no.
- Admitting a gap ("I haven't done this before") is not weakness, hiding it and
  improvising is what actually creates a mess.
- When someone else's forecast or claim is put under pressure, ask what it's
  based on before pushing back; that question does the work for you.

## Principles

- **Sell your opinion, don't just hold it.** Being right quietly changes
  nothing; senior stakeholders expect assertiveness and read passivity as a
  gap in the working relationship, not diplomacy.
- **Analyse stakeholders before deciding how assertive to be.** Establish early
  who can and can't be said no to, then be assertive with the ones who can.
  This is a triage step, not a blanket licence to push back on everyone.
- **Negotiate always, even when leverage feels thin.** The instinct to assume
  there's no hope is usually wrong; ask, because the answer might be needed
  more than expected, and the downside of asking is only a no.
- **Come clean on a gap rather than cover for it.** Confidently saying "I
  haven't specifically done this before, walk me through it" heads off the
  spiral of justifying an assumption that was wrong from the start.
- **Address issues directly; they don't resolve themselves by being ignored.**
  Covering up a possible misunderstanding lets it compound instead of closing
  it early.
- **When pressure-tested on a forecast or a deliverable's value, put it back
  on the other side first.** "What are you basing this on?", "Is that outside
  your expectations?" gets the real basis on the table before you commit to a
  position.
- **Asking for ideas is collaboration, not an admission of not knowing.**
  Brainstorming early, facilitated well, reads as a strength; hiding a gap in
  understanding to avoid looking unprepared is what actually looks bad later.
- **Can-do and pushing your own agenda aren't opposites.** A trusted advisor
  shows can-do and still asserts their own view; pure compliance reads as
  junior, not senior.

## Guidelines

- Before agreeing to something reflexively, ask whether it's a calculated
  favour or just avoiding the discomfort of saying no.
- When a forecast or ask feels unrealistic, question its basis before either
  accepting it or refusing it outright.
- Treat a capability gap as information to disclose early, not a risk to
  manage by staying quiet.
- If a deliverable's value hasn't been articulated, say so and ask for it,
  rather than producing it anyway to avoid the awkward conversation.

## Limitations

This model covers whether and how to push back once you're in the exchange. It
does not cover whether to take the commitment on in the first place, forecast
it, or assess its risk; that's [[mm-evaluate-before-committing]], the sibling
model that took the over-commit and risk half of the same source material.
This model also doesn't cover quantified negotiation outcomes (a specific
figure won or saved); those are filed against their own financial context, not
here.

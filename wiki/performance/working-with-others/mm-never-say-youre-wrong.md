# MM: Never Say You're Wrong

This is the Never Say You're Wrong mental model: correction only lands when it's
offered as an option that saves face, never as a verdict on the other person.
Read it before you correct, push back on, or challenge someone's approach,
especially someone with authority or standing. Full context and evidence are in
[[influencing-and-selling-ideas]].

**One-liner:** A correction only lands as an option that saves face.

**Reach for it when:** you're about to correct someone, or push back on their
approach, and want the change without the argument.

## Key Takeaways

- However right you are, telling someone they're wrong starts an argument you
  can't win.
- Frame the correction as one of several options for a shared problem.
- Let them take the exit; apologise for how something landed, not that you
  raised it.

## Principles

- **Criticism creates defensiveness; suggestion creates movement.**
- **Present alternatives as options for a shared problem**, not as a verdict on
  their approach.
- **Give them a face-saving exit** rather than making the point explicitly, even
  when you're clearly right.
- **Yes to the person, no to the task**: disagree with the request, not the
  person.

## Guidelines

- Before raising a correction, ask: does this look like I'm telling them they're
  wrong?
- Offer two or three options rather than one alternative that reads as a
  challenge.
- If a correction has already landed badly, apologise for how it was handled,
  not that it was raised, then rebuild from there.

## Limitations

Doesn't work when the other party needs an unambiguous no, a compliance or
safety issue, for instance. Face-saving framing can blur a message that has to
be direct.

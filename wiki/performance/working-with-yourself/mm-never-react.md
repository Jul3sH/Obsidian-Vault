# MM: Never React

This is the emotional-regulation half of the old combined "never react" pattern: the
discipline of putting a gap between a trigger and a response. It was split from the
epistemic half (checking whether the trigger was even true), which is now its own
model, [[mm-verify-before-acting]]. Read it before responding to anything that has
made you angry, defensive, or embarrassed.

**One-liner:** Between the trigger and the response, walk away.

**Reach for it when:** you feel the pull to respond right now, in the heat of a
message, meeting, or confrontation.

## Key Takeaways

- Reacting in the heat of the moment consistently makes things worse; it hands the
  other person the narrative and lowers you to their level.
- Silence and a cooling-off period are active strategies, not weakness or avoidance.
- Respond with facts and a forward plan once calm, never blame-shifting or venting,
  however justified it feels in the moment.
- The pattern repeats everywhere: insubordination, hostile emails, senior-management
  run-ins, a partner's frustration.

## Principles

- Walk away first, physically if you can (gym, sleep, a walk). Distance creates
  mental space before distance creates regret.
- Silence is a response. Giving someone no ammunition is often the strongest move
  available.
- Respond with facts and a forward plan, not blame-shifting or venting, however
  justified it feels in the moment.
- Seniority raises the stakes: reacting when you hold rank reads as losing control,
  not asserting it.
- If you did overreact, repair it plainly: apologise for the handling, not the
  underlying position, and move on.

## Guidelines

- Draft it, then leave it for an hour before sending.
- Ask "has anything actually happened here yet?" before composing a response.
- Take a deliberate cool-off period before a senior or high-stakes conversation
  rather than walking in hot.
- Catch defensiveness in real time: if you're justifying instead of listening,
  pause and listen first.

## Limitations

This model governs the emotional gap between trigger and response, not whether the
underlying claim is true. Check the facts too, that's [[mm-verify-before-acting]].
And walking away has a shelf life: letting a concern fester past its usefulness is a
different failure, covered in [[mm-setbacks-are-glitches]].

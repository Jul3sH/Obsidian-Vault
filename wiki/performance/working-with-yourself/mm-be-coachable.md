# MM: Be Coachable

This is the Coachability mental model: the moment the urge to correct or interrupt a
coach shows up, it is a sign of ego, not of a genuinely better answer. It exists
because this was a MISSED lesson: the source left its own "lesson learnt" line blank,
so this is the first time the pattern has been written down. Read it before a
coaching or mentoring session where you already suspect you know better than the
coach.

**One-liner:** You hired the coach; interrupting them to correct them is paying to
not listen.

**Reach for it when:** in a coaching, mentoring, or feedback session, and the urge to
interrupt or correct the person giving it shows up.

## Key Takeaways

- The urge to interrupt and correct a coach comes from an "I'm smarter than you"
  stance, not from the coaching actually being wrong.
- Wanting to correct the coach is itself the signal being coached against; noticing
  the urge is more useful than acting on it.
- Coaching only works if it is received; interrupting to correct it converts a
  listening exercise back into a performance for your own benefit.
- This sits next to, but is distinct from, general active-listening discipline: it
  is specifically about resisting the ego-driven urge to correct someone whose job in
  that moment is to be listened to.

## Principles

- **Notice the stance, not just the urge.** The interrupt-to-correct impulse comes
  from believing you're smarter than the coach; naming that stance out loud (even
  just to yourself) breaks its grip faster than trying to suppress the urge itself.
- **Being coachable is a discipline applied in the room, not a trait you either have
  or don't.** It has to be actively chosen each time the urge shows up.
- **If the coach is actually wrong, that's a separate, later conversation.** Correct
  it after hearing them out, not by interrupting mid-flow; the two rarely need to
  happen at the same moment.

## Guidelines

- When the urge to interrupt a coach or mentor arrives, let them finish first. Write
  the correction down if it needs to be said, and raise it once they're done.
- Ask what the "I'm smarter than you" stance is protecting. Usually it's the
  discomfort of being told something you'd rather have figured out yourself, not an
  actual factual error.
- Pay attention to who in the room is visibly getting more out of the coaching than
  you are; if they're taking it in and you're rehearsing rebuttals, that's the tell.

## Limitations

This model does not say every piece of coaching is correct, only that interrupting
it to correct it in the moment is rarely the right response. It does not cover
choosing a coach or deciding when to stop paying for one; both are separate
decisions from how to behave once in the session.
